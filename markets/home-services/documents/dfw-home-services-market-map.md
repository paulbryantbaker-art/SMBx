# Dallas–Fort Worth Home Services

## A Buy-Side Assessment for Acquirers · Published 3 August 2026

**HVAC · plumbing · electrical · roofing · pest control · garage doors.**

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **The acquirable universe is roughly 300 businesses, not 2,400.** Dallas–Fort
  Worth counts 2,412 plumbing-and-HVAC establishments (Census CBP, 2023) — and
  1,797 of them, 74.5%, have fewer than ten employees. The buyable middle is
  307 establishments of 10–249 employees.
- **The licence registry overstates the market roughly 2x.** TDLR licenses
  4,665 HVAC contracting firms in these eleven counties; only the CBP employer
  count describes businesses with payroll. Most licensed contractors have no
  employees at all.
- **Eighteen platform parents hold verified DFW ground** — ten residential-side,
  eight commercial-mechanical — and the map moved inside 2026: United Building
  Solutions entered in January, and Chemed re-acquired Roto-Rooter's Fort Worth
  territory in March.
- **Platform share of the acquisition band is 8.5% to 22.6%,** depending on
  where the band is drawn, and likely near the top of that range. What is left:
  roughly 280 establishments carrying an estimated $2–6B of annual work,
  matched to no consolidator.
- **Platform density runs exactly with recurring-revenue quality.** Pest
  control: 9 parents holding ≈40 locations — 10.1% of every establishment in
  the trade. HVAC and plumbing: 1.1%. Residential electrical service: no
  dedicated consolidator — one multi-trade recapitalization is the nearest
  exception. The consolidators' collective behaviour maps the annuity, trade by
  trade.
- **No primary source publishes metro revenue for construction trades.** Every
  "DFW HVAC market is worth $X billion" figure in circulation is derived,
  whether its publisher says so or not. The derived figures in this report say
  so, and carry their assumptions.

# PART I — THE METRO IN THE TEXAS MARKET

## Four metros hold four-fifths of the state

Texas plumbing-and-HVAC contracting (NAICS 238220) runs 8,909 establishments,
105,086 employees and $7.355B of annual payroll across 173 counties (Census
CBP, 2023). Four metros dominate it:

| MSA | Establishments | Employment | Annual payroll | Share of TX payroll |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Dallas–Fort Worth–Arlington** | **2,412** | **31,980** | **$2.418B** | **32.9%** |
| Houston–Pasadena–The Woodlands | 1,909 | 25,783 | $1.906B | 25.9% |
| Austin–Round Rock–San Marcos | 825 | 12,459 | $0.961B | 13.1% |
| San Antonio–New Braunfels | 822 | 8,927 | $0.563B | 7.7% |

Combined, the four hold 5,968 establishments — 67.0% of the state count — and
79.5% of its payroll. Dallas–Fort Worth is the largest home-services market in
Texas, 27% bigger than Houston on payroll, and it is the subject of this
report. The boundary throughout is the eleven-county MSA as the Office of
Management and Budget delineates it: Collin, Dallas, Denton, Ellis, Hunt,
Johnson, Kaufman, Parker, Rockwall, Tarrant and Wise counties.

## What can be measured at a metro, and what cannot

A word on method, because it separates this report from most metro market
figures in circulation. Establishment counts, employment, payroll and the full
size distribution are published at county level by the Census Bureau and are
carried here as measured. Revenue is not: the Economic Census publishes
construction receipts at state level and no lower, for any construction trade,
in any year. Every metro revenue figure in this report is therefore an
allocation from a measured state total, labelled as derived, with its
assumption and its range stated in the Derivations appendix. Ownership is
established from each platform's own published roster or a dated press release,
cross-checked against the state licence registry — never from directories or
data brokers.

# PART II — MARKET STRUCTURE: HVAC & PLUMBING

## The size distribution is the market

The single most important structural fact in Dallas–Fort Worth home services is
how small almost everyone is:

| Employment size class | Establishments |
|---|---|
| Under 5 | 1,330 |
| 5–9 | 467 |
| 10–19 | 291 |
| 20–49 | 192 |
| 50–99 | 66 |
| 100–249 | 49 |
| 250–499 | 7 |

**1,797 establishments — 74.5% — have fewer than ten employees** (Census CBP,
2023). These are owner-operator businesses: one to three trucks, no second
layer of management, no acquirable operating platform. They are the market's
long tail and its labour pool, but they are not its acquisition targets.

The buyable middle — 10 to 249 employees — is 307 establishments, of which 115
have twenty or more. The top of the market is seven establishments of 250-plus
employees. Metro-wide, 238220 employs 31,980 people on $2.418 billion of annual
payroll, averaging 13.3 employees per establishment.

## The licence registry, and the non-employer half

The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation offers a second, independent
census of the same trade: 4,665 distinct firms hold current air-conditioning
contractor licences in these eleven counties. Of those, 2,806 carry the
residential Environmental Air endorsement, and 1,738 hold Class B licences —
capped by statute at 25 tons of cooling capacity, which makes them
residential-scale by law rather than by anyone's classification.

Set the two counts side by side and the gap is the finding. The licence
registry counts nearly twice as many firms as the Census counts employer
establishments, in a code that also contains plumbing. The difference is the
non-employer population: most licensed contractors in this metro have no
payroll at all. **A market-sizing exercise that starts from the licence roster
overstates the acquirable universe by roughly 2x before any size screen is
applied.** Because Texas licenses plumbing under a separate agency, the TDLR
file is also a clean HVAC population — a separation the federal industry code
itself cannot make.

# PART III — THE DOLLAR DIMENSION

## Measured at the state, derived at the metro

Texas 238220 receipts were $25.418B in the 2022 Economic Census, 8.5% of the US
total of $297.609B. That state figure is measured. The metro figure is not
published and never will be, so it is allocated: on DFW's 32.87% share of state
238220 payroll, metro receipts come to **≈$8.356B a year — a derived figure.**
Allocating on employment gives $7.735B; on establishment count, $6.882B. The
21% spread across the three bases is the honest size of the assumption, and
this report carries the range rather than splitting it.

Receipts per employee statewide run $241,879 (2022 receipts over 2023
employment) — consistent with the ≈$180K–$271K per-employee range that platform
transaction reporting implies, and the first corroboration of that benchmark
from a federal statistical source.

## The trade split inside the code

NAICS 238220 bundles HVAC with plumbing. The Economic Census kind-of-business
detail separates them, at state level, as a measured split: HVAC contractors
take 34.3% of Texas 238220 receipts ($8.710B), plumbing contractors 32.8%
($8.333B), mechanical contractors 17.7% ($4.492B), with the remaining
commercial-leaning categories at 24.3% combined. Applied to the metro
allocation, DFW HVAC-specific spend is ≈$2.9B and plumbing ≈$2.7B — derived
twice over, and labelled accordingly.

What no public source separates, in any geography, is service work from new
construction inside this code. The recurring-revenue logic that underwrites
trade consolidation lives in service and replacement; that split remains an
assumption in every underwriting model built on this market, this report's
included.

## The map moved inside 2026

Three developments this year, all in the direction of more consolidation, not
less. United Building Solutions entered the metro by acquiring DFW Mechanical
Group of Wylie on 2026-01-20 (BusinessWire). Chemed re-acquired Roto-Rooter's
Fort Worth franchise territory on 2026-03-31 — bundled with San Francisco at
approximately $20.6 million, expected to add $5.0 million to $5.5 million of
2026 revenue (Chemed 8-K, 2026-04-23) — converting ground a screen would have
read as independently owned into company-owned territory.

And Apex Service Partners, the largest residential HVAC platform in the
country, relocated its headquarters into the metro: the Apollo announcement of
2026-05-28 is datelined "TAMPA, FL and DALLAS-FORT WORTH, TX." Apex describes
75 brands across 46 states and more than 13,000 employees, and publishes no
brand roster; no Apex operating brand anywhere in Texas could be identified
from public sources, including the state licence file. The independents of this
metro cannot be assumed Apex-free, and the residual figures in Part V are
overstated by whatever Apex owns here.

## The franchise layer

Thirty-seven franchise businesses trade in the metro under six national
nameplates — Aire Serv, Mr. Rooter and Mr. Electric (Neighborly), One Hour
Heating & Air Conditioning, Benjamin Franklin Plumbing and Mister Sparky
(Authority Brands). Twenty-five fall inside NAICS 238220 — 13 HVAC, 12
plumbing. A franchisee is independently owned and acquirable, with a franchise
agreement attached: neither platform-owned nor cleanly independent, and any
banding of this market owes it a line of its own.

# PART V — THE ACQUISITION BAND, AND WHAT IS LEFT

## The platform share, honestly bounded

Of the 307 establishments in the 10–249 employee band, approximately 25 are
platform-owned. Baker Brothers — 400 employees by its employer-reported count —
sits above the band entirely, one of the metro's seven establishments of
250-plus employees, and the first named placement of a platform location in a
specific Census size class.

Drawn at 10–249 employees, the platform share of the band is 8.5% of
establishments. Drawn at 20–249, it is 22.6%. The range cannot be narrowed
from public sources, because branch-level headcount is not published for
privately held trade businesses — the four platform locations whose headcounts
are published (Baker Brothers 400, Berkeys 195, Lex 50+ technicians, Dallas
Mechanical Group more than 150) all exceed fifty employees, which is why the
truth likely sits near the top of the range.

## What is left

Roughly 280 establishments in the 10–249 band match no consolidator on a
register of sixty verified parents. The band as a whole carries an estimated
$3.6B – $6.4B of annual work; the unmatched residual, roughly $2–6B — bounds
computed from the size-class structure, not point estimates, and registered in
the Derivations appendix.

Two qualifications keep those figures honest. "Unmatched" means not found on
the register — a business whose ownership changed without public record would
still read as independent. And the residual is overstated by the Apex position
described in Part IV. Neither qualification changes the read: this is not a
closed metro, and it is not an open one. It is tuck-in territory, with the
tuck-in ground now measured for the first time — roughly 280 businesses and
several billion dollars of annual work.

# PART VI — THE ADJACENT TRADES

## The density gradient

The same bottom-up cut applied to the metro's other home-services trades
produces the most thesis-relevant structure in this report:

| Trade | Estabs | Employment | Payroll | Receipts | Platform presence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electrical (238210) | 1,634 | 28,980 | $2.149B | ≈$6.880B derived | ≈8 commercial large-owner locations; no residential-first platform found |
| Roofing (238160) | 907 | 6,053 | $0.458B | ≈$2.759B derived | commercial held; residential/storm entries 2024–2025 |
| Pest control (561710) | 396 | 3,954 | $0.206B | **$585.1M measured** (2022 Economic Census) | ≈40 locations across 9 parents |
| Finish carpentry (238350), the garage-door host code | 391 | 3,260 | $0.194B | ≈$0.845B derived | Guild Garage Group confirmed absent |

**Platform density runs exactly with recurring-revenue quality.** Pest control —
where residential recurring share runs 85.4% — is the one home-services trade
with a measured metro market: $585.1M of 2022 receipts across 344 firms, of
which DFW holds 32.9% of the Texas total. It carries ≈40 platform locations
against 396 establishments: 10.1% of the entire trade, held by Rollins
(including HomeTeam Pest Defense, headquartered in Dallas, and Romex, acquired
2026-04-02), Rentokil with ten-plus Terminix branches, Anticimex through a
three-company entry announced 2025-06-16, and six more owners. HVAC and
plumbing sit at 1.1%. Residential-first electrical: zero. The consolidators'
collective revealed preference maps the annuity, trade by trade — and tells an
acquirer which trades the market has already decided are worth owning.

## Roofing: overweight, and episodic

Dallas–Fort Worth carries 39.58% of Texas roofing payroll against 32.87% of its
plumbing-and-HVAC payroll — the metro is overweight roofing, and the overweight
is hail. It is also the most fragmented trade in the cut: 89.1% of roofing
establishments have fewer than ten employees. Commercial roofing is held by
five national platforms (Tecta America twice over, Nations Roof, CentiMark,
Flynn Group, FirstService's Roofing Corp of America); the residential and
storm-restoration side has drawn dated sponsor entries through 2024 and 2025
without density yet.

One gate belongs in front of any roofing thesis: receipts run 6.03x payroll in
this trade, against 3.46x in HVAC — subcontracted-crew economics — and
storm-restoration revenue is insurance-event-driven and episodic. The annuity
logic that underwrites HVAC service agreements does not transfer to a hail
market by default.

## The empty side: residential electrical service

Electrical is the inverse of pest — with a precision worth stating carefully. A
larger trade than roofing by establishments (1,634) and nearly HVAC's equal in
payroll ($2.149B), it has **no dedicated residential service-and-repair
electrical consolidator** in the metro. What does exist, named exactly: the
multi-trade platforms sell electrical as a service line (Wrench Group's Baker
Brothers and Berkeys); one residential multi-trade operator with an
electrical-led brand — **Team Enoch** — was recapitalized by McKinney Capital, a
field-services family investment firm, in March 2022 and has since expanded to
Austin and Houston, the nearest thing this segment has to a platform; the
new-construction channel is consolidated by **IES Residential**, a public
holding company's residential-electrical division with a Rowlett branch; and
the commercial side belongs to large operators (Facility Solutions Group,
Rosendin, Comfort Systems' Walker Engineering) plus twelve franchise locations.

The segment is not empty of scale — its largest electrical-led residential
operator is a substantial family-held multi-brand group, and that is the point:
**the size exists; the institutional capital, with one exception, does not.**
Whether that is an opening or a warning turns on a question the data does not
answer: whether residential electrical service can carry the recurring-revenue
annuity that has pulled consolidators into every trade where it exists. One
recapitalization in four years may itself be the answer.

# PART VII — THE READ FOR A BUYER

## Where the openings are

Three, in order of conviction. First, HVAC-and-plumbing tuck-ins: roughly 280
unmatched establishments in the buyable band, in the largest home-services
metro in Texas, where every major platform has already accepted that entry is
by tuck-in. An acquirer with an anchor here is buying in a market whose
consolidation logic is proven and whose remaining inventory is now measured.
Second, residential and storm roofing — the overweight trade with sponsor
entries accelerating and no density yet — for a buyer who has underwritten the
episodic-revenue problem rather than assumed it away. Third, and most
cautiously, residential electrical: unconsolidated, structurally adjacent to
trades the platforms already run, and unproven as an annuity.

## What would make us walk

The labour constraint binds hardest here: 31,980 employed in the core trade
against a replacement-demand market, in a state where every metro competes for
the same technicians. A buyer whose model requires hiring its way to growth is
underwriting the tightest input in the market. Second, the Apex unknown: a
platform of 75 brands with its headquarters now in Irving and no published
roster could hold materially more of this metro than any public source shows —
the Part V residual is a ceiling, not a floor. Third, for any thesis leaning on
the derived dollar figures: the 21% allocation spread is real, and a buyer
pricing entry on ≈$8.356B should know that $6.882B is an equally defensible
reading of the same data.

## A.4 How to read the figures, and where they came from

**Everything in this report is drawn from publicly available information.** The
sources are U.S. government statistical releases, SEC filings and issuer
disclosures, published market-research and trade-association output, trade and
financial press, and the public websites and press releases of the companies
named. Each is cited where it is used and listed in full in the Sources section
below.

**No proprietary or confidential information was obtained or used in preparing
this report.** Nothing here derives from a confidentiality agreement, a data
room, management access, or a client engagement. No company named in this
document provided information for it.

**Every figure in the body sits in one of three classes.** The body does not
label them, because a reader should not have to read a footnote to reach a
sentence. The convention is:

| Class | What it means | How to recognise it |
|---|---|---|
| **Reported** | A named publisher states the figure. Government series, filings, research houses, trade press. | The publisher and date appear alongside it. |
| **Verified** | Checked against the primary source on the publication date — the table, the release, the filing itself. | Also carries its publisher; the verification record lives in the research files. |
| **Derived** | Arithmetic on reported figures, not published anywhere. | Registered in Derivations below with inputs, working and assumption. |

**One distinction the body does observe, because it changes what a number
means.** Where a transaction figure reaches print through trade press citing
unnamed sources, with the parties declining to disclose terms, it is public but
it is not a disclosure. The outlet is named wherever one appears.

**On the valuations shown.** Enterprise values are either figures reported by a
named outlet or estimates produced by the stated framework with its assumptions
exposed. Neither is an appraisal, and nothing here should be read as a valuation
of a business a reader might transact with. Market-level multiples and ranges
are market context, not target pricing.

**Where the figures are least stable.** Platform ownership and brand rosters
change monthly in this market; the 2026 entries in Part IV are the proof. The
CBP vintage is 2023 and refreshes annually; the Economic Census is quinquennial
with 2022 the current base.

**The correction record.** Corrections are recorded in A.0.1 above rather than
quietly absorbed. Each entry states what the figure was, what it became, and
the named source that overturned it.

## What we don't know yet

- **What Apex owns in this metro.** The largest single unknown in the report.
- **Employee counts for most platform locations** — the input that would turn
  the 8.5–22.6% range into a figure.
- **Service versus new construction inside 238220** — confirmed unpublished in
  every public geography (the last candidate Census table splits by location of
  work, not work type). Permanently an assumption, and stated as one.
- **The roofing replacement-versus-storm mix** — the number that decides
  whether the overweight trade is an annuity or an episode.

- **Who owns what in Houston, Austin and San Antonio.** The ownership work in
  this report covers Dallas–Fort Worth only; the other metros are measured in
  Part I and unmapped beyond it.
