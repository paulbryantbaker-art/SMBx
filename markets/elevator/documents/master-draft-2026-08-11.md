## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**The one-sentence version.** Elevator service is the best-protected recurring-revenue
annuity in the building trades — maintenance is compelled by code, not chosen by the
customer — and it is also the trade where the incumbent manufacturers hold the most
structural power over anyone trying to buy into it. Both things are true, and the
second is why the multiple has not run away.

**Six findings that decide whether this is a lane.**

1. **Maintenance is code-compelled, and so is modernization.** ASME A17.1 §8.6.1.2
   requires a written, unit-specific Maintenance Control Program for every unit in
   service; an owner cannot lawfully operate without one **[D]**. Separately and more
   powerfully, **NFPA 101 §9.4.2.2 requires existing elevators to conform to ASME
   A17.3** — the retroactive code — and **CMS adopted the 2012 Life Safety Code, with
   tag K531 on its survey form**, so every Medicare- or Medicaid-participating
   facility in the country is inside that requirement **whether or not its state
   adopts A17.3** **[D]**. Twelve states adopt it directly on top of that. This is a
   stronger legal basis than anything in home services.

2. **The OEMs hold roughly half the service base and the better half.** Otis alone
   maintains ~2.5m units globally **[D]**, and its own 10-K concedes independents hold
   about **50% of service units** — explicitly the lower-value half **[D]**. Otis's
   service segment runs a **25.1% operating margin against 4.8% on new equipment — a
   20.3pp gap** **[D/E]**. The service annuity is where all the profit is, and the
   OEMs know it.

3. **The lock-in is documented, and it is lawful.** *In re Elevator Antitrust
   Litigation* (2d Cir., 06-3128-cv, 4 Sep 2007) records the mechanism in the
   plaintiffs' own words — refusal to sell competitors "the parts, tools, software or
   diagrams," and "embedded computer systems that can only be interfaced with
   defendant-controlled handheld units" — **and affirms dismissal** **[D]**. No
   successor case. No right-to-repair statute reaches elevators. This is the single
   most important fact for an acquirer: the constraint on an independent's route book
   is real, permanent, and has already survived antitrust challenge in the US.

4. **The posted "~10% of US elevator units" claim cannot be traced and is retired
   here.** See **A.0.1**. The magnitude survives as our own bottom-up estimate —
   **8–9%** **[E]** — but no published source states it.

5. **The market is consolidating from a low base and PE is currently a net seller.**
   Ten PE-backed service platforms verified. But Elevated went to APi (a strategic) on
   4 Jun 2024, and eight of Urban Elevator's eleven US sites went to Otis on
   21 Apr 2025 **[P]**. The sponsors who built platforms are exiting into strategics
   and OEMs.

6. **A structural event is in flight.** **KONE agreed to acquire TK Elevator on
   29 April 2026, EV €29.4bn, combined ~3.2m maintained units, closing no earlier than
   Q2 2027, with a contractual provision for regulator-forced divestments** **[D]**.
   Forced divestment of service routes by a merging OEM pair is the largest potential
   source of acquirable density this market has seen, and the timing is knowable.

**Where the opening is.** Below roughly **750 contracted units**, no sponsor process
exists at all **[E, from the consolidator register's own floors]** — and that
population concentrates in the Interior Mountain West, Northern Plains, Interior
South/Appalachia and Northern New England, where no platform has presence.

**What would make us walk.** Labor. Elevator mechanics are the highest-paid
construction trade in the BLS series and the trade is heavily unionised under a
national agreement with a **1:1 hard cap on helpers and apprentices per mechanic**
**[D]**. There is no labor-arbitrage thesis here. A buyer who underwrites this like
HVAC will be wrong by a wide margin.

# PART I — WHY THIS MARKET, AND WHY THE USUAL FRAME FAILS

## 1.1 This is not a home-services trade and must not be underwritten as one

Every instinct carried over from HVAC, plumbing and roofing is wrong here in a
specific, checkable way.

| Dimension | Home services | Elevator | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recurring revenue | Customer *chooses* a service agreement | Code *requires* a Maintenance Control Program **[D]** | The annuity does not churn on a price objection |
| Labor cost | Arbitrage-able; median HVAC tech **$57,300** **[D]** | Median $102,420, 90th pct $138,910 **[D]** | No labor lever. Wages are the moat *against* you |
| Labor supply | Open shop, hire and train | Union, 1:1 apprentice cap, 4-year NEIEP programme **[D]** | You cannot buy growth by hiring |
| Incumbent | Fragmented independents | Five global OEMs holding ~half the base **[D]** | Your competitor is a company with $14,431 million of revenue |
| Parts | Commodity distribution | Proprietary controllers, lawful refusal to supply **[D]** | Some route books are not contestable at any price |
| Consolidation stage | Mid-cycle, PE buying | Early, and **PE currently selling** **[P]** | Exit path is strategics and OEMs, not sponsor-to-sponsor |

The comparison that settles it: **the elevator median wage ($102,420) sits within 1%
of the plumber 90th percentile ($103,140), and above the HVAC 90th percentile
($84,250)** — same BLS vintage, same table **[D]**. The median elevator mechanic
out-earns nearly every plumber in the country.

## 1.2 What we are actually buying

A route book. Not a business with a route attached — the route *is* the asset. The
diligence question is not "what does this company earn" but "how many units, under
what contract terms, on whose controllers, at what visit frequency, with what
callback load."

Three of those five inputs are not in any published dataset. They are diligence
inputs, and Part IX says so plainly.

# PART II — MARKET SIZING, AND WHY IT CANNOT BE BOUGHT

## 2.1 The container problem — read this before using any 238290 figure

**Elevator and escalator work has no dedicated NAICS code.** It sits inside **238290
Other Building Equipment Contractors**, whose illustrative examples include vending
machines, ATMs, bowling alley equipment and church bells **[D]**.

We can size the contamination exactly, which is unusual and worth doing:

| Vintage | Elevator/escalator mechanics (SOC 47-4021) | Total 238290 employment | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| BLS OEWS May 2022 | 20,640 | 148,470 | **13.90%** **[D/E]** |
| BLS OEWS May 2023 | 21,300 | 153,170 | **13.90%** **[D/E]** |

The second-largest occupation in the bucket is mechanical insulation workers (14,810)
**[D]**. **Roughly 86% of NAICS 238290 is not elevator work.** Two independent
vintages returning 13.90% is a stable result, not a coincidence.

**Therefore: every 238290 figure in this document is a CONTAINER figure and is
labelled as one. None of them is an elevator figure.**

## 2.4 The installed base — one source, and it is dated

Every US elevator count in circulation traces to **NEII** (National Elevator Industry,
Inc.), and NEII's own fact sheet says so:

- 2020 fact sheet: **>1.03m elevators**, **56,000 North American escalators** **[D]**
- 2019 fact sheet: **35,000 US escalators / 44,000 US+Canada** **[D]** — which
  conflicts with the 2020 sheet. **Both values carried. No midpoint.**
- The 2019 sheet states: **"A majority of this data was compiled in 2007."** **[D]**

So the denominator under every share calculation in this market is roughly
**nineteen years old**. That is not a reason to discard it — it is the only figure
there is — but every share computed against it inherits that vintage, and this
document says so at each use.

**The one independent bottom-up anchor found:** Los Angeles's regulator reported
**23,700 permitted conveyances, of which 20,406 elevators**, in a January 2018 audit
**[D]**. A state-registry sample is the way to build a real US count, and Part VIII
demonstrates the method on New York.

## 2.5 Published market sizes do not survive arithmetic

Two vendor figures were checked and both failed:

- **IBISWorld: US elevator-only market $53.8bn (2024)** **[E]** — larger than the
  entire NAICS 238290 container ($42.4bn, 2022) **[D]**, of which elevator work is
  ~14% by employment. The two cannot both be right. Its two stated CAGRs also imply

- **Arizton's installed-base series** implies **~1,183 units retired per year — a

**The practical consequence: market size for this sector has to be built bottom-up —
from device registries, contract pricing and route density — rather than purchased.**

# PART III — THE ANNUITY, UNDERWRITTEN

This is the strongest part of the document and the reason the market is interesting.

## 3.1 Maintenance is compelled, not chosen — the MCP

**ASME A17.1 §8.6.1.2** requires a written, unit-specific **Maintenance Control
Program**: scheduled tasks, procedures, examinations, tests and as-built wiring
diagrams **[D]**. **ASME's own A17.2-2010 refers to "the maintenance control program
(MCP) as required by 8.6.1.2.1"** — the requirement is confirmed from an ASME
document, not only from regulators quoting it, and real A17.1 code text on the MCP
was reached in the 2005 addenda to the 2004 edition.

> **Two sub-citations are withdrawn pending settlement (A.0.2).** Earlier drafts
> attributed **five-year on-site record retention to §8.6.1.4.1** and **callback logs
> to §8.6.1.4.2**. In the edition actually read, those two sections govern *record
> contents* and *availability to elevator personnel* — not retention period and not
> callbacks. Searches for "retained", "on site" and "callback" found neither at those
> numbers. Either both entered at a later edition under the same numbering, or the
> triangulation mis-mapped them. **The MCP requirement itself is unaffected.** Record
> retention and callback logging are real obligations in this trade and are relied on
> in diligence; they are simply not cited to a section number here until one is
> confirmed.

An owner cannot lawfully operate a unit without one. That converts maintenance from a
discretionary operating expense into a compliance obligation.

- **Origin:** entered ASME A17.1 at the **2000 edition**, from CSA B44 S1-97 **[P]**
  (Koshak, *Elevator World*).
- **Enforcement, corroborated in states' own binding text:** Illinois OSFM quoting
  A17.1-2007 §8.6.1.2 — *"A written Maintenance Control Program shall be in place"*
  **[D]**; Florida 61C-5.0015 enforcing §8.6.1.2 with deadlines **[D]**.

> **Verification caveat, stated prominently.** A17.1 itself is paywalled and was not
> read verbatim in this session — UpCodes is robots-blocked and direct retrieval was
> blocked by egress policy. **Every section number above is triangulated from
> regulators citing it, not read from the code.** Pass 6 must read A17.1 directly.
> This is the highest-priority verification item in the document.

## 3.3 Third-party independence — four regimes, no national rule

**This decides whether third-party inspection is a standalone business or a captive
line, and the answer is: it depends entirely on the state.**

| Regime | States | What it means for a buyer |
|---|---|---|
| **Independence mandated** | **NYC** (Admin Code §28-304.6.1 — *"not affiliated with the agency performing the maintenance,"* statutory, not rule); **Illinois/Chicago**; **Maryland** (COMAR 09.12.81.04-1); **Texas** (employer-level conflict bar) **[D]** | A real, protected inspection market exists. Inspection and maintenance are separate businesses by law |
| **No private market at all** | MA, WA, HI, PA, NJ — **public inspectors** **[D]** | Inspection is not acquirable. Do not underwrite it |
| **Silent / captive** | GA, DC, MS, OH (only via certifier ethics) **[D]** | The maintenance provider inspects its own work. Inspection is a line item, not a business |
| **Individual-only bar** | Kansas — bars the *individual*, not the firm **[D]** | A firm may inspect its own units through a different employee |

**And the political exposure.** **NEII is on record opposing mandatory independence** —
*"properly regulated self-inspection should not be construed as a conflict of
interest"* (2013) **[D]**. The industry's own trade body is lobbying against the rule
that creates the independent inspection market. Anyone underwriting third-party
inspection is underwriting a regulatory position the incumbents are actively working
to reverse.

**Buy-side implication:** third-party inspection is a genuine business in a minority
of states, and a politically contested one. It is not the core thesis. It is an
adjacency, and it should be scoped state by state or not at all.

## 3.4 A17.3 — the retroactive capex engine, partially mapped

**A17.1 governs new installations. A17.3 (Safety Code for Existing Elevators and
Escalators) is retroactive** — where adopted, existing units must be brought to a
safety baseline whether the owner wants to or not. **That makes A17.3 adoption the
engine behind modernization demand.**

**No published national adoption table exists.** Checked: ASME, NEII, NAESA, Justia.
Built from primary code instead:

| State | Edition | Instrument | Retroactive effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Florida** | A17.3-2020 | Rule 61C-5.001 | §3.10.12 door-contact monitoring moved from 12/31/2023 to **8/1/2025**, enforcement deferred to **8/1/2029** **[D]** |
| **Georgia** | A17.3-2020 | Rule 120-3-25-.02(c)(3) | A real schedule: §3.10.12 due **1/1/2025** (OCI Bulletin 23-EX-5), 2-year door restrictors, 5-year hydraulic §4.3.3 **[D]** |
| **Illinois** | A17.3-2005 | 41 Ill. Adm. Code 1000.60 | upgrades *"completed by January 1, 2015"* **[D]** |
| **Michigan** | A17.3-2017 | R 408.7003 | adopted **[D]** |
| **New York City** | 2002 vs 2015 — **both carried, unresolved** | Appendix K, Ch. K3 | deadline **1/1/2020** **[D]** |
| **Texas** | A17.3-2002 | 16 TAC §74.100 | *"continues to be in effect"* **[D]** |
| **Washington** | A17.3-2015 | WAC 296-96-00650 | effective 10/1/2018 **[D]** |
| **Colorado** | adopted | 7 CCR 1101-8-2-7 | **stock installed before 7/1/2008 is exempt** — largely defanged **[D]** |
| **Idaho** | A17.3-2015 | | adopted **[D]** |
| **Ohio** | A17.3-2020 | OAC 4101:5-3-01, eff. 7/1/2024 | **⚠ ADOPTED BUT NOT RETROACTIVE.** OAC 4101:5-3-02(B) expressly bars retroactive application *"to existing elevators that are not otherwise being altered or repaired"* — **Ohio forces no capex** **[D]** |

**Twelve states adopt A17.3** (the ten above plus Chicago and Vermont, both from
summary sources only). **Ohio and Colorado are the cautionary pair: adoption alone
does not mean forced capex**, and a map that counts adopters without reading the
retroactivity clause overstates demand.

> **A trap worth naming.** **NEII's February 2019 table is an A17.1 tracker whose
> A17.3 column is blank for six states that do adopt it.** Absence from that table
> means nothing at all. Anyone building an adoption map from it will produce a
> confident undercount.

**Five confirmed negatives:** Oregon (absent from OAR 918-400-0455), New Jersey,

**Eleven states remain UNDETERMINED**, each with the source checked and no answer

**Twelve adopters across at least six different editions — 2002, 2005, 2015, 2017,

**No published cost figure was found for any retroactive item.** The recommended

Illinois's retroactive list is the concrete one, and it is what modernization actually

> Medicaid-participating facility in the United States is therefore inside the
> A17.3 requirement **regardless of whether its state adopts A17.3 at all**. Texas
> cites it by section number at 26 TAC §505.164.
>
> Three independent evidence classes: the code text in two editions, a federal
> survey instrument, and state administrative code citing it. **Scope conditions
> to respect: "except as modified herein", and Chapter 9 binds through the
> occupancy chapters — only §28.5.3.1 was captured verbatim, so the full occupancy
> map is not yet established.**

## 4.3 The lock-in — documented, tested in court, and lawful

The hardest available evidence is **In re Elevator Antitrust Litigation, 2d Cir.,
06-3128-cv, 4 September 2007** **[D]**, which records the mechanism in the complaint's
own words:

> refusing to sell competitors "the parts, tools, software or diagrams"
>
> "embedded computer systems that can only be interfaced with defendant-controlled
> handheld units"

**And then affirms dismissal**, because no prior course of dealing had been terminated.

Three consequences an acquirer must hold simultaneously:

1. **The mechanism is real and judicially described.** This is not an industry rumour.
2. **It is lawful in the United States.** No successor case. No right-to-repair
   statute reaches elevators.
3. **Therefore some route books are not contestable at any price** — and which ones
   depends on the controller population in the book, which is not published anywhere
   and is a **diligence input**.

**The European counter-case, for contrast:** European Commission decision of
21 February 2007, **COMP/E-1/38.823**, fines exceeding **€992m** across four
countries, with scope expressly covering *"sale, installation, maintenance and
modernisation"* **[D]**. (Per-company fine allocations were not verified and are left
empty.) Europe treated the conduct as cartel behaviour; the US Second Circuit
dismissed the analogous complaint. **Do not carry the European precedent as if it
governs US conduct.**

## 4.4 Where the OEMs are structurally weak — the opening

Two disclosures from the incumbents themselves define the whitespace:

- **Otis's 10-K concedes independents hold ~50% of service units** — and characterises
  that half as **the lower-value half** **[D]**.
- **Schindler's CFO** attributed an Americas service decline to *"increased
  selectivity when it comes to recaptures"* **[D]** — the incumbent is choosing not to
  chase certain units back.

Read together: the OEMs concentrate on high-value, high-density, newer-equipment
routes, and are deliberately conceding low-density, older, geographically dispersed
units. **That concession is the acquirable market.** It is also, by the OEMs' own
characterisation, the lower-margin half — which must be underwritten honestly rather
than treated as an oversight.

**IUEC membership: 31,290, "year covered 2025"** — obtained via the Center for Union
Facts republishing an LM-2 **[P]**.

> **Source-interest disclosure, and it is not optional here.** The Center for Union
> Facts is an anti-union advocacy organisation. The underlying LM-2 is the primary

> corroborates the magnitude. **Pass 6 must read LM-2 file 000-197 directly.**

**The NEBA national agreement (2022–27)**, signed by all seven major OEMs **[D]**:

- annual increases of **3.45–3.50%**
- a **1:1 hard cap on helpers and apprentices per mechanic**
- overtime straight to **double time**

**The 1:1 cap is the most important operating constraint in this document.** It means

**So an acquirer cannot buy growth by hiring.** Capacity expands at the rate the
apprenticeship pipeline allows, and that pipeline is jointly controlled. Growth comes
from acquiring route books with mechanics attached — which is precisely why this is a
consolidation market and not an organic-growth market.

# PART VII — OPERATING ECONOMICS AND THE DEAL RECORD

**This is the section the practice's own rules flag as the highest fabrication risk in
the job.** Everything below is sourced or left empty. Several slots are left empty.

## 7.1 Contract pricing — real documents, not benchmarks

Two public-sector contracts give real per-unit pricing **[D]**:

- **Wisconsin state contract 19-5971: $141 – $1,347.77 per unit per month**, with
  visit frequency priced explicitly (weekly / semi-monthly / monthly).
- **Bay St Louis: ~$134 per unit per month**, quarterly visits, **parts excluded**.
  Terms: 3-year, auto-renewing for successive 3-year periods, 30-day notice,
  **escalation capped at ≤5%**.

**The 9.6× spread between $141 and $1,347.77 is not noise — it is visit frequency.**
Sourced contracts run from **4 to 52 visits per year, a 13× span** **[D]**. Any
"revenue per unit" figure without a visit frequency attached is meaningless.

Derived range: **$1,611 – $16,173 revenue per unit per year** **[E]**, from those two
contracts. Elevated: **~$367k revenue per employee** **[E]**.

**Contract terms more broadly:** practitioner sources describe 3–5 year terms with
90–120 day cancellation windows and rights of first refusal **[P]**. **No US statute
or case governing elevator contract terms was found.** Notably, the 2004 SDNY
antitrust complaint alleges maintenance **bid-rigging** but contains **no allegations
about term, renewal or cancellation** — checked directly, and worth recording because
the "notoriously restrictive elevator contract" is widely asserted and thinly
evidenced.

## 7.2 Contract tiers — the reframe that matters

The instinctive model is that full-maintenance contracts include more work than
lube-and-examine contracts. **That is wrong, and the code is why.**

**A17.1's MCP mandate requires identical documented tasks and intervals at every
tier** (per Colorado DOPS) **[D]**. **Tiers do not differ in the work performed. They
differ in who absorbs parts and callbacks.**

The callback arithmetic, which is the whole risk transfer **[E]**:

- Traction units average **4 callbacks/year** (Schloss, via Koshak) **[P]**
- At the verified California loaded rate of **$108.095/hr**, with the NEBA
  **double-time** overtime rule
- **At the low end of sourced contract pricing, callbacks alone consume 54–107% of
  contract revenue**

**That single computation explains the market's contract structure.** It is why cheap
contracts exclude parts, and why Bay St Louis's contract specifies "7am–4pm weekdays"
— restricting hours is how a low-price contract survives the overtime rule.

**For a buyer:** a route book priced at the bottom of the range with parts included
and no hours restriction is not a bargain, it is a liability. **Contract tier mix is a
first-order diligence item**, ahead of revenue.

## 7.3 Route density — mostly unsourced, and honestly so

**Units per mechanic: ~34–39 nationally** **[E]** — corroborated across three
independent denominators (BLS employment, IUEC membership, NEIEP enrolment).

> **A correction carried forward.** An earlier research file treated ~34–39 as a
> ceiling. **It is a floor.** The denominator includes installation and modernization
> workers who carry no route, and the numerator rests on a 2007-vintage unit count.
> Both errors push the true route figure **higher**. File `05` has this backwards and
> this document supersedes it.

**The circulating "65–90 units per technician per day" figure is not usable.** Its
origin is **CT Acquisitions, an M&A advisory that sells elevator deal advisory**
**[P]** — interest disclosed — and **its attribution to NAEC was checked at naec.org
and falsified.** It is also implausible on its face against a 4-visits-per-year
contract structure.

**No point benchmark is published, and there is a good reason:** units per mechanic is
a *dependent variable* of contracted visit frequency. With visit frequency spanning
13×, a single ratio cannot exist. **The correct diligence question is units × visit
frequency per mechanic, not units per mechanic.**

**Retention:** Otis reported **93.5% → 92.4%** and said returning to 94% "will take
sustained time" (Marks, Q4 2024 call) **[D]**. Note this is the *OEM's* retention on
its own installed base — the best case in the market, and it is declining.

## 7.4 Valuation — and why no LMM comp exists

**Derived from primary filings** **[E]**:

- **APi / Elevated: ~12.95×** — $570m ÷ ($220m × 20% assumed margin). **The 20% margin
  is an assumption, not a disclosure**, and the multiple moves with it.
- **KONE / TK Elevator: ~18.2×** — €29.4bn ÷ €1,617m adjusted EBITDA.

**Otis public trading multiple**, both values carried, no midpoint **[D]**:

- EV/EBITDA **14.20** (GuruFocus, 11 Aug 2026)
- EV/EBITDA **14.71** (stockanalysis / S&P, 10 Aug 2026)

> **None of these is a lower-middle-market comparable and none may be used as one.**
> Two are multi-billion-dollar strategic transactions; the third is a global public
> company. Applying any of them to a $5–50m independent is the error this warning
> exists to prevent.

**No disinterested elevator-specific LMM multiple exists, and we established why.**
Elevator sits in **NAICS 238290 — the code containing vending machines, ATMs, bowling
alley equipment and church bells**. **GF Data and DealStats therefore cannot produce
an elevator multiple**; they can only produce a contaminated 238290 blend, **which is
more dangerous than nothing because it carries a real NAICS code and reads as
authoritative.**

**Disinterested floor context only, explicitly not a comp:** BizBuySell Q2 2026,
**2.7× SDE**, N≈50,000, voluntary broker reporting, CoStar-owned, **no elevator
category** **[P]**.

**Who would have to publish one for it to exist:** GF Data adding sub-NAICS tagging;
NAEC (confirmed to publish **no** benchmarks at all); or a strategic disclosing an
add-on purchase price. **Until one of those happens, this practice should decline to
quote an elevator LMM multiple and should say why — which is a differentiator, not a
gap.**

# PART IX — AGGREGATION CHALLENGES

**1. The controller question is the central diligence item, and it is unanswerable
from public data.** Whether a route book is contestable depends on the controller
population in it. Nobody publishes that. It has to come from the target's own asset
register, and a seller who cannot produce one is telling you something.

**2. Unit counts are never public.** Zero of ten platform About-pages published one.
The unit book *is* the asset. Treat unit count as a diligence input; never screen on it.

**3. Contract tier mix outranks revenue.** Per §7.2, callbacks can consume 54–107% of
revenue at the bottom of the pricing range. Two books with identical revenue can have
opposite economics.

**4. Growth is capped by the apprenticeship pipeline, not by capital.** The NEBA 1:1
cap means you cannot hire your way to capacity. Every incremental mechanic is either
acquired with a book or produced by a four-year programme whose completion rate is
unpublished.

**5. The OEMs bid against you and may buy from you.** Six dated transactions in §4.5.
This cuts both ways and should be underwritten as both.

**6. PE is currently a net seller.** The exit path that home-services buyers assume —
sponsor-to-sponsor — is not the observed pattern here. Observed exits are to
strategics (APi) and OEMs (Otis).

**7. No LMM comp exists and the available proxies are contaminated.** §7.4. A 238290
blend from GF Data or DealStats carries a real NAICS code and is more dangerous than
having nothing.

**8. The denominator is nineteen years old.** Every share statistic in this market
rests on NEII data described by NEII as majority-compiled in 2007.

# PART X — BUY-SIDE IMPLICATIONS

## 10.1 The two bands

Paul's instruction, 2026-08-11: a platform band and a tuck-in band, scored separately.
The register's own behaviour supplies the boundary — **below ~750 contracted units no
sponsor process exists.**

**PLATFORM BAND**

| Attribute | Specification |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $10m–$100m |
| Units | 750+ contracted, ideally 2,000+ |
| Contract mix | Full-maintenance majority; parts-included priced accordingly |
| Density | Building-concentration profile, not just unit count (§8.3) |
| Geography | One metro with genuine density, or a contiguous two-metro pair |
| Workforce | Mechanics attached and staying; IUEC relationship intact |
| Segment mix | Service-weighted. Install/mod heavy books are a different business |
| Exit | Strategics (APi class) and OEMs — both demonstrated with dates |

**TUCK-IN BAND**

| Attribute | Specification |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $2m–$15m |
| Units | 150–750 |
| Why it works | Below the sponsor floor, so no competitive process |
| Requirement | Must fold into an existing route — a standalone dispersed book is a trap |
| Owner situation | Succession-driven. ~94% of BLS openings are replacement demand; the operator population is ageing with the mechanic population |
| Watch | Controller mix, contract tier, callback history |

## 10.2 Where to hunt

1. **The KONE–TKE remedy window (§4.6)** — the highest-value item, and time-boxed.
   Position a client before the remedy phase, not after the announcement.
2. **The four whitespace regions (§5.3)** — Interior Mountain West, Northern Plains,
   Interior South/Appalachia, Northern New England — *with* the counter-argument in
   §5.3 tested, not assumed.
3. **Modernization, and it is bigger than an adoption map suggests (§3.4)** — the
   NFPA 101 → CMS path makes A17.3 compliance national for health-care facilities
   regardless of state adoption, and twelve states adopt it directly on top. **Health
   care is therefore the single best-defined modernization demand pool in this
   market.** Read the retroactivity clause before sizing any state: Ohio adopts A17.3
   and expressly bars retroactive application, and Colorado exempts everything
   installed before 1 July 2008.
4. **Third-party inspection, in the four mandate states only** (§3.3) — and priced for
   the political risk that NEII is lobbying against the mandate.

## 10.3 Underwriting guardrails

- **No labor-arbitrage assumption.** §6.1.
- **No LMM multiple quoted.** §7.4. Decline, and explain why — it differentiates.
- **No revenue-per-unit figure without visit frequency attached.** §7.1.
- **No share statistic without stating whether OEM sponsors are in the denominator.** §5.1.
- **No modernization sizing until NFPA 101 §9.4 is settled.** §3.4.
- **Unit count is never a screening input.** §5.2.

## 10.4 What would make us walk

- The controller mix in target books turns out to be predominantly proprietary and
  recent — the route is not contestable and the annuity is the OEM's, not the target's.
- The whitespace regions turn out to be dispersed-route traps, and the health-care
  modernization pool turns out to be already served by the OEMs — the two openings
  close at once.
- The KONE–TKE review closes with no service divestments.
- A client requires organic growth. The 1:1 cap makes that structurally unavailable.

## Derivations

**D-1 — PE share of US elevator units, bottom-up.** PE-backed roll-up unit counts, where
published, sum to approximately 79,000–96,000 (range reflects platforms with no
published count, which are excluded rather than estimated). Denominator: NEII >1.03m US
elevators (2020 fact sheet). 79,000 ÷ 1,030,000 = **7.67%**; 96,000 ÷ 1,030,000 =
**9.32%**. Stated as **8–9%**. *Assumptions, stated as limits: seven platforms publish
no unit count and are excluded, so this is a floor; the denominator is majority-compiled
2007 per NEII's own note; and PE-owned OEMs (TK Elevator, Advent/Cinven) are EXCLUDED —
including them would change the answer by an order of magnitude.*

**D-2 — Otis service margin gap.** 25.1% − 4.8% = **20.3pp**. Ratio 25.1 ÷ 4.8 =
**5.23×**. FY2024 gap 18.5pp, so movement = 20.3 − 18.5 = **+1.8pp**.

**D-3 — Otis service share of segment profit.** $2,374 million ÷ $2,614 million = **90.8%**, against
service being 35% of sales.

**D-4 — Otis US share.** $4,192 million ÷ $14,431 million = **29.1%**.

**D-5 — KONE installed-base share.** (EUR 4,753.6 million + EUR 2,394.0 million) ÷ EUR 11,245.2 million = **63.6%**.

**D-6 — TKE installed-base share.** (€4.5bn + €1.6bn) ÷ €9.2bn = **66.3%** computed
against **65%** stated. *Both carried; the gap is rounding in the stated components.*

**D-7 — Elevator mechanic share of NAICS 238290 employment.** May 2022: 20,640 ÷
148,470 = **13.90%**. May 2023: 21,300 ÷ 153,170 = **13.91%**. Stated as 13.90%.

**D-8 — NYC record-count overstatement.** 120,256 ÷ 93,454 = **1.287**, i.e. record
count overstates the active base by **28.7%**, stated as 29%.

**D-9 — NYC device concentration.** 32,208 ÷ 93,454 = **34.5%** of devices in 3,072 ÷
43,454 = **7.1%** of buildings. 32,208 ÷ 3,072 = **10.5** devices per building.

**D-10 — NYC compliance workload.** ≈**108,321** statutory inspection events per year,
from the active base against the 12/60-month CAT1/CAT5 structure. Validation: theoretical
CAT5 rate 16,246/yr against observed 2024 filings 16,212 = **0.2%** variance.

**D-11 — NYC devices per licensed contractor.** 92,075 ÷ 132 = **697**, stated as ~700.
*Assumption stated as a limit: this counts licensed contractors only and does not
allocate the OEM branches' disproportionate books.*

**D-12 — APi/Elevated implied multiple.** $570m ÷ ($220m × 20%) = **12.95×**. *The 20%
EBITDA margin is an ASSUMPTION, not a disclosure. At 15% the multiple is 17.3×; at 25%
it is 10.4×. The multiple is not robust and should be quoted with this range or not at
all.*

**D-13 — KONE/TKE implied multiple.** €29.4bn ÷ €1,617m = **18.18×**, stated as 18.2×.

**D-14 — Callback cost against contract revenue.** 4 callbacks/yr at the CA loaded rate
$108.095/hr under the NEBA double-time rule, against the low end of sourced contract
pricing ($141/unit/month = $1,692/unit/yr). *Assumptions: callback duration and travel
are not sourced, which is why the result is a range (54–107%) and not a point. This is
directional evidence for the tier-mix argument, not a costing.*

**D-16 — The two size bands.** Neither band is a sourced figure; both are this
practice's own specification, and they are registered here so they are never mistaken
for findings. The boundary is the one observed fact behind them: **below ~750
contracted units no sponsor process was found** in the consolidator register. The
platform band ($10 million–$100 million revenue) sits above that floor where a
competitive process must be assumed; the tuck-in band ($2 million–$15 million) sits
below it, where one need not be. *Assumption stated as a limit: the 750-unit floor is
an observed absence across the platforms we could verify, not a published threshold —
absence of evidence across 27 parents, not a stated policy.*

**D-15 — Elevator wage against other trades.** $102,420 ÷ $103,140 = **0.993** — within
**1%** of the plumber 90th percentile. $102,420 ÷ $84,250 = **1.216**, i.e. the elevator
median exceeds the HVAC 90th percentile by **21.6%**. $102,420 ÷ $57,300 = **1.79×** the
HVAC median. *All four inputs are BLS OEWS May 2023, same vintage and same table.*

**D-17 — NAICS 238210 establishment count.** 203,401 (all of 2382) − 122,152 (the
238220 + 238290 residual) = **81,249**. *Assumption stated as a limit: this is arithmetic
on two 2022 Economic Census figures, not a third published figure, and it is carried only
so the comparison row is not blank.*
