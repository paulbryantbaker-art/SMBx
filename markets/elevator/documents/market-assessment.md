# Elevator & Escalator Service: A Buy-Side Assessment

## A Buy-Side Assessment for Acquirers · Published 11 August 2026

**Maintenance and repair · modernization · new installation · third-party inspection.**

1. **The recurring revenue in this trade is compelled by code, not chosen by the customer.** A building owner cannot lawfully operate a unit without a written Maintenance Control Program, and existing units must additionally conform to a retroactive safety code — nationally, through a route most operators have never traced.
2. **The incumbent manufacturers earn a 25.1% operating margin on service against 4.8% on new equipment**, and they hold that annuity behind a lock-in that has already survived antitrust challenge in the United States.
3. **Two of the five global manufacturers are merging**, with a contractual mechanism for regulator-forced divestments and a review window running through 2027. That is the largest block of acquirable maintenance density this market has offered.

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

- **Maintenance is a compliance obligation, not a service contract.** ASME A17.1 §8.6.1.2 requires a written, unit-specific Maintenance Control Program — scheduled tasks, procedures, examinations, tests and as-built wiring diagrams — for every unit in service. ASME's own A17.2-2010 refers to "the maintenance control program (MCP) as required by 8.6.1.2.1," and states enforce it directly: Florida 61C-5.0015 attaches deadlines to it. On top of that sits a fixed testing clock, reproduced identically in six states' own binding rules: **Category 1 at 12 months, Category 3 at 36 months, Category 5 at 60 months**, with periodic inspection annually. New Jersey requires escalator inspection every six months. Recurring revenue a code requires is a different asset from recurring revenue a customer renews.

- **Modernization is mandated nationally, and almost nobody frames it that way.** Nine states adopt ASME A17.3 on their own instruments, with New York City and Chicago adopting separately as cities. That undersells it. **NFPA 101 §9.4.2.2 requires existing elevators to conform to A17.3**, and CMS adopted the 2012 Life Safety Code — its survey form CMS-2786R carries tag K531, "Existing elevators conform to ASME/ANSI A17.3." Every Medicare- or Medicaid-participating facility in the country therefore sits inside the requirement regardless of what its state adopts. Texas cites the section by number at 26 TAC §505.164. Health care is the best-defined modernization demand pool in this market.

- **Read the retroactivity clause before sizing any state.** Adoption alone does not force capital expenditure. Ohio adopts A17.3-2020 at OAC 4101:5-3-01 and then expressly bars retroactive application at OAC 4101:5-3-02(B) — "not to be retroactively applied to existing elevators that are not otherwise being altered or repaired." Colorado exempts everything installed before 1 July 2008. Georgia and Florida, by contrast, carry real dated schedules. An adoption count read as demand overstates the market.

- **The service annuity is where all the profit is, and the manufacturers know it.** Otis Worldwide reported FY2025 service revenue of 9,442 against new equipment of 4,989 ($m), at operating margins of **25.1% and 4.8%** — a 20.3 percentage-point gap that widened 1.8 points from FY2024. New equipment was 35% of sales and produced **9%** of segment operating profit; service was 65% of sales and produced **91%**. KONE's installed-base businesses are 63.6% of sales; TK Elevator's are 65% as stated. The manufacturers are not equipment companies with a service arm.

- **The constraint on an independent's route book is real, permanent and lawful.** *In re Elevator Antitrust Litigation* (2d Cir., 06-3128-cv, 4 September 2007) records the mechanism in the plaintiffs' own words — refusal to sell competitors "the parts, tools, software or diagrams," and "embedded computer systems that can only be interfaced with defendant-controlled handheld units" — and affirms dismissal, because no prior course of dealing had been terminated. There is no successor case and no right-to-repair statute reaches elevators. Which route books are contestable depends on the controller population inside them, and that is published nowhere.

- **There is no labour arbitrage in this trade, and a buyer arriving from home services will underwrite the wrong cost line.** BLS OEWS (May 2023) puts the elevator mechanic median at **$102,420** against an HVAC median of $57,300 — 1.79 times — and within 1% of the *ninetieth percentile* for plumbers. California's prevailing determination loads a $63.95 basic rate to a **$108.095** total package. The trade is unionised under a national agreement whose ratio rule caps helpers, apprentices and assistant mechanics against mechanics on any one job, and mechanics come out of a four-year, 6,800-hour apprenticeship. Capacity is bought with route books, not with hiring.

- **A structural event is in flight and it is roughly time-boxed.** KONE agreed to acquire TK Elevator on 29 April 2026 at an enterprise value of **€29.4 billion**, combining approximately **3.2 million** maintained units, with completion no earlier than the second quarter of 2027. Consideration adjusts "based on the terms and scope of any potential divestments of TKE's or KONE's current business operations required for the satisfaction of regulatory conditions to completion." Forced disposals by a merging pair are the largest source of acquirable density this market has produced, and the window is knowable.

- **The acquirable ground sits below the sponsors' floor.** Ten private-equity-backed service platforms were verified, alongside four adjacent holdings and seven strategic consolidators. Below roughly **750** contracted units no sponsor process was found at all, and no platform presence was found in the Interior Mountain West, the Northern Plains, the Interior South and Appalachia, or Northern New England. That absence is an opportunity only if the route-density arithmetic in Part V survives contact with a specific book.

# PART I — THE MANDATE

## 1.1 The Maintenance Control Program

The distinguishing fact of this trade is that its recurring revenue rests on a legal obligation rather than a commercial relationship.

**ASME A17.1 §8.6.1.2 requires a written, unit-specific Maintenance Control Program** covering scheduled tasks, procedures, examinations, tests and as-built wiring diagrams. An owner cannot lawfully operate a unit without one. The requirement is confirmed from an ASME document rather than only from regulators quoting it: **ASME A17.2-2010** refers to "the maintenance control program (MCP) as required by 8.6.1.2.1," and real A17.1 code text on the MCP was reached in the 2005 addenda to the 2004 edition.

States enforce it directly. Florida 61C-5.0015 attaches compliance deadlines to §8.6.1.2. The practical effect for an acquirer is that a maintenance contract in this trade is not discretionary spend that disappears in a downturn — it is the mechanism by which a building meets a code obligation.

Two sub-citations that earlier drafts of this practice's research carried — record retention at §8.6.1.4.1 and callback logging at §8.6.1.4.2 — are withdrawn. In the edition read, those sections govern record *contents* and *availability to elevator personnel*. Record retention and callback logging are real obligations relied on in diligence; they are simply not cited to a section number here until one is confirmed.

## 1.2 The testing clock

The annuity has a fixed rhythm, and it reproduces identically across six states' own binding rules rather than resting on the model code alone.

| Test | Interval | Verified in |
|---|---|---|
| Periodic inspection | 12 months | CA 8 CCR §3141.6; NYC Admin Code §28-304.6.1 |
| Category 1 | 12 months | CA, NYC, TX TDLR, DC DOB, RCW 70.87.120, Hawaii Exhibit C |
| Category 3 | 36 months | same |
| Category 5 | 60 months | same |
| Escalator inspection, New Jersey | 6 months | NJ rule |

Six independent state citations returning the same 12/36/60 structure is a strong result. Every unit generates a mandated billable event annually and a larger one every five years, and Part VI computes what that workload amounts to in a single metropolitan area.

## 1.3 Modernization is mandated nationally, not in a handful of states

Nine states adopt **ASME A17.3** on their own instruments — the retroactive safety code for *existing* elevators and escalators — with New York City and Chicago adopting separately as cities. Counting jurisdictions understates the mandate substantially.

**NFPA 101 §9.4.2.2 requires existing elevators, escalators, dumbwaiters and moving walks to conform to ASME A17.3.** The companion provision, §9.4.2.1, points *new* installations at ASME A17.1/CSA B44 and is verified verbatim. The consequence runs through federal healthcare regulation: **CMS adopted the 2012 Life Safety Code**, and its survey instrument **CMS-2786R carries tag K531 — "Existing elevators conform to ASME/ANSI A17.3."** Every Medicare- or Medicaid-participating facility in the United States therefore sits inside the A17.3 requirement, whatever its state does. Texas cites the section by number at 26 TAC §505.164.

Three independent evidence classes support this: the code text in two editions, a federal survey instrument, and state administrative code citing it. Two scope conditions apply and should be respected — the section opens "except as modified herein," and Chapter 9 binds through the occupancy chapters, of which only §28.5.3.1 was captured verbatim.

Ohio and Colorado are the cautionary pair. Ohio adopts the code and then bars its retroactive application to existing elevators "that are not otherwise being altered or repaired." Colorado exempts the majority of its installed base by vintage. Five states are confirmed non-adopters — Oregon, New Jersey, Iowa and West Virginia, with Massachusetts running its own A17-2004 modifications — and eleven remain undetermined, including California and New York State.

Illinois's retroactive list is the concrete picture of what modernization means in practice: door restrictors, car illumination, emergency signalling, phase reversal protection, door reopening devices, pit stop switches and pit ladders, with firefighters' operation and hydraulic cylinders carved out under §4.3.3(b) unless the controller is altered, fails, or presents a safety risk.

One trap is worth naming because it produces a confident undercount. **NEII's February 2019 table is an A17.1 tracker whose A17.3 column is blank for six states that do adopt it.** Absence from that table means nothing at all.

## 1.5 Third-party inspection — four regimes, no national rule

Whether inspection is a standalone business or a captive line depends entirely on the state.

| Regime | States | What it means for a buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Independence mandated | NYC (Admin Code §28-304.6.1 — "not affiliated with the agency performing the maintenance"), Illinois/Chicago, Maryland (COMAR 09.12.81.04-1), Texas | A protected inspection market exists; inspection and maintenance are separate businesses by law |
| No private market | Massachusetts, Washington, Hawaii, Pennsylvania, New Jersey — public inspectors | Not acquirable. Do not underwrite it |
| Silent or captive | Georgia, DC, Mississippi, Ohio | The maintenance provider inspects its own work; a line item, not a business |
| Individual-only bar | Kansas | A firm may inspect its own units through a different employee |

The regime is also politically contested. **NEII is on record opposing mandatory independence** — "properly regulated self-inspection should not be construed as a conflict of interest" (2013). Anyone underwriting third-party inspection is underwriting a regulatory position the incumbents are working to reverse. It is an adjacency to be scoped state by state, not a core thesis.

Certification runs under ASME QEI-1. **Three ANAB-accredited certifying organisations operate under it**, not one, so a roster count from any single body undercounts. No population figure and no age profile is published anywhere reachable, and there is no BLS occupation code for elevator inspector — they sit inside SOC 47-4011. The ageing-inspector finding that carries the fire and life safety thesis has no verified equivalent here and should not be assumed.

**No manufacturer discloses a United States or Americas unit count.** OEM share of the American installed base therefore cannot be computed, and this assessment does not estimate one.

Otis's 10-K states that independent service providers "have an aggregate portfolio of about 50% of service units, but account for a smaller percentage of the service business when measured by value." That sentence sits under *Competition*, is scoped "in most of our local geographies," and is unchanged across five consecutive 10-K filings. **It is a global statement and is not a measurement of the United States.** No verified US split of manufacturer versus independent share exists.

## 2.3 The lock-in, and why it is permanent

*In re Elevator Antitrust Litigation*, Second Circuit, docket 06-3128-cv, decided 4 September 2007, records the mechanism in the complaint's own words: refusal to sell competitors "the parts, tools, software or diagrams," and "embedded computer systems that can only be interfaced with defendant-controlled handheld units." **The court affirmed dismissal**, because no prior course of dealing had been terminated.

An acquirer must hold three consequences at once. The mechanism is judicially described rather than rumoured. It is lawful in the United States, with no successor case and no right-to-repair statute reaching elevators. And therefore **some route books are not contestable at any price** — which ones depends on the controller population inside them, a fact published nowhere and available only from a target's own asset register. A seller who cannot produce one is telling you something.

The European record is a weaker analogue than its headline suggests. The Commission's decision of 21 February 2007, COMP/E-1/38.823, covers four countries with fines summing to €992,312,200 — but the Official Journal carries an express qualification for the largest single component: "with the exception of Germany where — to the knowledge of the Commission — services were not directly part of the cartel agreements." Germany is €617 million of that total. The European case is a poor precedent for a service thesis and should not be carried as one.

## 2.4 Where the manufacturers concede ground

Two disclosures from the incumbents define the opening. Otis characterises the independent half of the service base as accounting for a smaller share by value. Schindler's chief executive describes deliberate selectivity about which units to win back.

Read together, the manufacturers concentrate on high-value, high-density, newer-equipment routes and consciously concede low-density, older, geographically dispersed units. **That concession is the acquirable market** — and it is, by the incumbents' own characterisation, the lower-margin half. It must be underwritten honestly rather than treated as an oversight.

## 2.5 The manufacturers are both a competing bidder and an exit

The manufacturers buy independent route books directly, which makes them a competing bidder against any platform a client builds and a credible exit for one. The APi transaction adds a third class of buyer — a large non-manufacturer strategic — and it was the exit for a sponsor-owned platform.

## 2.6 The KONE–TK Elevator combination

**KONE agreed to acquire TK Elevator on 29 April 2026**, at an enterprise value of **€29.4 billion including interest-bearing net debt**, combining approximately **3.2 million** units under maintenance. TKE is disclosed at approximately €20.5 billion in annual sales. Completion is subject to regulatory approvals **and KONE shareholder approval**, earliest in the second quarter of 2027 — the shareholder condition is a real gate, since consideration includes new class B shares.

The provision that matters to a buy-side practice is the divestment mechanism. Consideration "would be adjusted in connection with completion, based on (i) the terms and scope of any potential divestments of TKE's or KONE's current business operations required for the satisfaction of regulatory conditions to completion," and KONE states it "is confident that it will secure all necessary regulatory approvals in accordance with its contractual commitments."

That is a consideration-adjustment mechanism keyed to divestments, confirming that defined remedy obligations exist. No scope, content or cap is disclosed, and there is no hell-or-high-water standard. Note that it expressly covers **KONE's own operations**, not only TKE's.

Two of five global manufacturers combining will draw competition review in the United States and Europe, and the parties have contracted for the possibility of forced disposals. **Divested service routes would be the largest block of acquirable maintenance density this market has offered.** A client positioned before the remedy phase — capital committed, geography stated — is in a materially better position than one reacting to an announcement.

# PART III — MARKET SIZING, AND WHY IT CANNOT BE BOUGHT

## 3.1 The container problem

Elevator and escalator work has **no dedicated NAICS code**. It sits inside **238290 Other Building Equipment Contractors**, whose illustrative examples include vending machines, automated teller machines, bowling alley equipment and church bells.

The contamination can be measured exactly, which is unusual and worth doing. BLS OEWS puts elevator and escalator mechanics (SOC 47-4021) at **13.90%** of NAICS 238290 employment in both May 2022 (20,640 of 148,470) and May 2023 (21,300 of 153,170). The second-largest occupation in the bucket is mechanical insulation workers. **Roughly 86% of NAICS 238290 is not elevator work**, and two independent vintages returning the same share is a stable result rather than a coincidence.

Every 238290 figure below is therefore a container figure and is labelled as one.

The 2022 Economic Census, table EC2223BASIC, NAICS 238290, employer establishments: establishments **7,725**; firms **6,611**; receipts **$42,421,304k**; employment **141,908**; annual payroll **$11,853,912k**.

## 3.2 Fragmentation, bounded rather than measured

Establishment counts by employment-size band for 238290 specifically are the table that would size acquirable inventory, and they were not obtained. What exists bounds the answer.

| Population | Establishments | Under 5 | Under 10 | Under 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NAICS 2382 — the parent | 203,401 | **52.59%** | **73.13%** | **86.65%** |
| NAICS 238210 — sibling | 81,249 | 52.80% | 73.18% | 86.40% |
| 238220 + 238290 residual | 122,152 | 52.45% | 73.10% | **86.82%** |
| All construction, CBP 2022 | 800,651 | **65.87%** | 81.71% | 91.02% |

The residual is an upper bound on 238290, not a measurement of it — it still contains all of plumbing and HVAC, which is far larger. The percentages are shares of the establishments operated the entire year, and that base must never be mixed with the full total.

One finding survives cleanly and it runs against instinct. **Building-equipment contractors are less fragmented than construction at large** — 52.59% under five employees against 65.87%. A buyer importing home-services fragmentation assumptions will overestimate small-firm inventory in this trade.

## 3.3 The installed base rests on one source, and it is dated

Every American elevator count in circulation traces to **NEII**, and NEII's own fact sheet says so. The 2020 sheet gives more than **1.03 million elevators** and **56,000** North American escalators. The 2019 sheet gives 35,000 US escalators and 44,000 across the United States and Canada, which conflicts with the 2020 figure; both are carried.

The 2019 sheet states: **"A majority of this data was compiled in 2007."**

The denominator beneath every share statistic in this market is therefore approximately nineteen years old. That is not a reason to discard it — it is the only figure there is — but every share computed against it inherits the vintage. The one independent bottom-up anchor located is Los Angeles's regulator, which reported 23,700 permitted conveyances of which 20,406 elevators in a January 2018 audit. Part VI demonstrates what a modern registry produces instead.

## 3.4 Published market sizes do not survive arithmetic

Two vendor figures were checked and both failed. IBISWorld's US elevator-only market of $53.8 billion (2024) is larger than the entire NAICS 238290 container at $42.4 billion (2022), of which elevator work is roughly 14% by employment; its two stated compound growth rates also imply 20.3% growth in 2020, a year the construction market did not have. Arizton's installed-base series implies approximately 1,183 units retired per year, a 924-year service life; its growth rate reconciles internally while its stock-and-flow does not.

Market size in this sector has to be built bottom-up — from device registries, contract pricing and route density — rather than purchased.

## 4.2 The share statistic that does not exist

No published source states a private-equity share of United States elevator units. This practice's own bottom-up estimate is **8–9%** — sponsor-backed roll-up unit counts, where published, against NEII's denominator — and it is an estimate rather than a finding, registered with its arithmetic in the Derivations table.

Two cautions attach. The figure decays, because sponsors are currently net sellers: Elevated exited to APi in June 2024 and eight of Urban Elevator's locations to Otis in April 2025. And it is denominator-ambiguous, because TK Elevator is sponsor-owned — **a private-equity share statistic in this market means nothing unless it states whether manufacturer sponsors are inside the denominator.**

# PART V — LABOUR AND OPERATING ECONOMICS

## 5.1 The wage picture, and the thesis it forecloses

BLS OEWS, May 2023, SOC 47-4021: employment **23,990**; median annual wage **$102,420**; mean **$100,060**; ninetieth percentile **$138,910**. The Occupational Outlook Handbook gives 24,200 jobs in 2024 at a median of $106,580, projected up 5% to 2034 on approximately 2,000 openings a year — of which roughly 94% is turnover rather than growth.

The same-vintage comparison is what forecloses the usual thesis. Against an HVAC median of **$57,300**, an HVAC ninetieth percentile of **$84,250** and a plumber ninetieth percentile of **$103,140**, the elevator median sits within 1% of the plumber ninetieth percentile, exceeds the HVAC ninetieth percentile by 21.6%, and runs 1.79 times the HVAC median. All four figures are OEWS May 2023 national.

California's prevailing wage determination SC-62-X-999-2023-1 loads a mechanic basic rate of **$63.95** per hour to a total package of **$108.095** per hour — a 69.03% fringe load. That determination expired 31 December 2023 and is a point-in-time reference rather than a current rate.

There is no labour arbitrage here. A buyer arriving from home services will underwrite a cost line roughly double what they expect, carrying a fringe load they have not seen.

## 5.2 Growth is capped by a pipeline, not by capital

The trade is unionised under the NEBA national agreement (2022–27), which carries annual increases of **3.45–3.50%** and takes overtime straight to double time. Its ratio rule caps the total of helpers, apprentices and assistant mechanics against mechanics "on any one job, except on jobs where two teams or more are working."

Mechanics come out of NEIEP: four years, 6,800 on-the-job hours, 144 classroom hours a year. **Annual completions are not published anywhere.**

The consequence for an acquirer is structural. Capacity expands at the rate the apprenticeship produces mechanics, and that pipeline is jointly controlled. **Growth comes from acquiring route books with mechanics attached** — which is precisely why this is a consolidation market rather than an organic-growth one.

IUEC membership is reported at 31,290 by a source this assessment does not rely on: the primary filing could not be retrieved, and the secondary carries no file number, report identifier or fiscal year. IUEC's own site states "30,000+ across the US and Canada." Note the two-country scope — juxtaposing a US-and-Canada union roll against a US-only BLS employment count would imply more than 100% density.

## 5.3 Contract pricing, and the tier question that outranks revenue

Two public-sector contracts give real per-unit pricing. Wisconsin state contract 19-5971 runs from $141 to $1,347.77 per unit per month, with visit frequency priced explicitly. Bay St Louis runs approximately $134 per unit per month for quarterly visits with parts excluded, on a three-year term auto-renewing for successive three-year periods, 30 days' notice, and escalation capped at 5%.

The 9.6-fold spread between those endpoints is not noise — it is visit frequency, and sourced contracts run from 4 to 52 visits a year. **Any revenue-per-unit figure without a visit frequency attached is meaningless.**

The instinctive model of contract tiers is wrong, and the code is why. A17.1's MCP mandate requires identical documented tasks and intervals at every tier. **Tiers do not differ in the work performed; they differ in who absorbs parts and callbacks.**

The arithmetic behind that is the whole risk transfer. Traction units average four callbacks a year. At the verified California loaded rate under a double-time overtime rule, callbacks alone consume **54–107%** of contract revenue at the low end of sourced pricing. That single computation explains the market's contract structure — why cheap contracts exclude parts, and why Bay St Louis specifies 7am to 4pm weekdays, since restricting hours is how a low-price contract survives the overtime rule.

**For a buyer: a route book priced at the bottom of the range with parts included and no hours restriction is not a bargain, it is a liability.** Contract tier mix is a first-order diligence item, ahead of revenue.

## 5.4 Route density, stated honestly

Units per mechanic runs approximately **34 to 39** nationally, corroborated across three independent denominators — BLS employment, IUEC membership and NEIEP enrolment. That is a floor rather than a ceiling: the denominator includes installation and modernization workers who carry no route, and the numerator rests on a 2007-vintage unit count. Both errors push the true figure higher.

A circulating figure of 65 to 90 units per technician per day is not usable. Its origin is an M&A advisory that sells elevator deal advisory, and its attribution to NAEC was checked and falsified — NAEC publishes no benchmarks at all.

No point benchmark is published, and there is a good reason: units per mechanic is a dependent variable of contracted visit frequency, and with frequency spanning thirteenfold a single ratio cannot exist. **The correct diligence question is units multiplied by visit frequency per mechanic.**

Otis reported retention moving from 93.5% to 92.4%, with management noting "some of that is involuntary." That is a manufacturer's retention on its own installed base — the best case in the market — and it declined.

## 5.5 Valuation, and the comparable that does not exist

Two multiples derive from primary filings. APi's acquisition of Elevated implies approximately 12.95 times on an assumed 20% margin — an assumption rather than a disclosure, and the multiple is not robust to it: at 15% it is 17.3 times, at 25% it is 10.4 times. KONE's agreement for TK Elevator implies approximately 18.2 times. Otis trades at EV/EBITDA of 14.20 (GuruFocus, 11 August 2026) and 14.71 (stockanalysis/S&P, 10 August 2026); both are carried.

**None of these is a lower-middle-market comparable and none may be used as one.** Two are multi-billion-dollar strategic transactions and the third is a global public company.

No disinterested elevator-specific lower-middle-market multiple exists, and the reason is structural. Elevator sits in NAICS 238290 — the code containing vending machines, automated teller machines, bowling alley equipment and church bells — so GF Data and DealStats cannot produce an elevator multiple. They can only produce a contaminated 238290 blend, **which is more dangerous than nothing because it carries a real code and reads as authoritative.**

For a floor reference only, and explicitly not a comparable: BizBuySell reported 2.7 times SDE in Q2 2026 across roughly 50,000 voluntary broker-reported transactions with no elevator category.

# PART VI — THE NEW YORK CUT

New York was selected on data availability rather than size. It publishes device-level open data that no other American metropolitan area matches, and everything below was computed from the registry rather than read from a publication.

## 6.1 The active base, and the 29% trap

| Measure | Devices |
|---|---|
| Total records | 120,256 |
| Active devices | **93,454** |
| Removed, dismantled, deleted, withdrawn, sealed, work in progress | 26,802 — 22.3% |
| Less construction equipment | 1,379 |
| Permanent vertical-transportation base | **92,075** |

Using record count in place of active count overstates the serviceable base by 29%. Any metropolitan figure built on "New York has 120,000 elevators" is wrong by that margin.

By type: elevators **81,232**; accessibility lifts 7,034; escalators **2,606**; dumbwaiters 1,197; moving walks 6. Escalators are 71.1% Manhattan, the most geographically concentrated segment in the data, and escalator and dumbwaiter decommission rates run at roughly twice the elevator rate — shrinking asset classes that should not be underwritten as growing ones.

By borough, of the 93,454 active base: Manhattan **48,378**, more than the other four combined; Brooklyn 20,115; Queens 13,333; the Bronx 9,693; Staten Island 1,935.

## 6.2 Route density is the economics, and here it is measurable

Across 43,454 buildings the mean is 2.15 devices, and the mean is useless. The distribution is the finding.

| Devices in building | Buildings |
|---|---|
| 1 | 25,946 |
| 2 | 9,786 |
| 3–5 | 5,460 |
| 6–10 | 1,407 |
| 11 or more | 856 |

**59.7% of buildings hold exactly one device, and those buildings hold only 27.8% of all devices. At the other end, 3,072 buildings — 7.1% — hold 32,208 devices, 34.5% of the base.** The threshold there is five or more devices; only about 1,000 buildings hold ten or more.

This is the economics of the trade in one table. A mechanic servicing a ten-device building has no travel time between units; a mechanic servicing ten single-device buildings spends the day driving. **Two route books of identical unit count can differ substantially in margin, and the building-concentration profile is what separates them.** In New York that question can be answered from public data before a conversation starts. Almost nowhere else can.

## 6.3 The live annuity, separated from the dormant base

| Measure | Devices | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Category 1 current, all types | 87,111 | **93.2%** |
| Category 1 stale | 4,652 | |
| Category 1 never filed | 1,691 | |
| Elevators, Category 1 current | | **96.1%** |
| Elevators, Category 5 within five years | 67,198 | **82.7%** |
| Elevators, Category 5 never filed | 8,442 | **10.4%** |

Approximately 10,000 devices sit outside the compliance annuity entirely — either a data-quality artefact or an enforcement gap, and worth knowing which. Either way the *live* maintained base is smaller than the active base, and this is the only American metropolitan area where that distinction can be measured.

Derived compliance workload runs to approximately **108,321** statutory inspection events a year. One validation is worth recording: the theoretical Category 5 rate of 16,246 a year matched observed 2024 filings of 16,212, a variance of 0.2%. The mandated annuity is being complied with at close to the rate the code implies, which is the strongest available evidence that it is real revenue rather than paper obligation.

## 6.4 Supply, and the honest limit

New York State Department of Labor licence counts: **132** active elevator contractors, **78** inspection agencies, 3,948 mechanics and 748 inspectors. Against roughly 92,000 devices that is approximately 700 devices per licensed contractor before accounting for the manufacturer branches that hold the largest books.

The registry covers the five boroughs only. The metropolitan statistical area extends into Westchester, Nassau, Suffolk, Rockland, Putnam and northern New Jersey, all governed separately and none in this data. **Roughly a third of the metropolitan area is unmeasured**, and any claim built on this data must say five boroughs.

# PART VII — BUY-SIDE IMPLICATIONS

## 7.1 The two bands

The register's own behaviour supplies the boundary: below approximately 750 contracted units, no sponsor process was found.

| | Platform band | Tuck-in band |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $10 million to $100 million | $2 million to $15 million |
| Units | 750 or more, ideally 2,000 | 150 to 750 |
| Why it works | scale enough to absorb tuck-ins | below the sponsor floor, so no competitive process |
| Contract mix | full-maintenance majority, priced accordingly | must fold into an existing route |
| Density | building-concentration profile, not unit count alone | a standalone dispersed book is a trap |
| Owner situation | institutional or founder | succession-driven; roughly 94% of BLS openings are turnover |
| Exit | strategics and manufacturers, both demonstrated with dates | absorbed into the platform |

## 7.2 Where to hunt

1. **The KONE–TK Elevator remedy window.** The highest-value item and the only one that is time-boxed. Position a client before the remedy phase rather than after an announcement.
2. **Health-care modernization.** The NFPA 101 to CMS path makes A17.3 compliance a federal condition of participation, independent of state adoption. It is the best-defined demand pool in the market.
3. **The four whitespace regions**, with the callback-economics counter-argument in Part V tested rather than assumed.
4. **Third-party inspection, in the four mandate states only**, priced for the risk that the industry's trade body is lobbying against the mandate.

## 7.3 Underwriting guardrails

- No labour-arbitrage assumption.
- No lower-middle-market multiple quoted. Decline, and explain why — it differentiates.
- No revenue-per-unit figure without a visit frequency attached.
- No share statistic without stating whether manufacturer sponsors sit in the denominator.
- No modernization sizing off adoption counts alone. Ohio and Colorado adopt A17.3 and force little or no capital expenditure.
- Unit count is never a screening input.

## 7.4 What would make us walk

- The controller mix in target books proves predominantly proprietary and recent — the route is not contestable and the annuity belongs to the manufacturer.
- The whitespace regions prove to be dispersed-route traps and the health-care modernization pool proves already served by the manufacturers, closing both openings at once.
- The KONE–TK Elevator review closes with no service divestments.
- A client requires organic growth. The apprenticeship pipeline makes that structurally unavailable.
