# Run 03 · Hunt B — The OEM Layer: revenue split, maintained-unit portfolios, and the lock-in mechanism

**Scope of this pass.** Otis, Schindler, KONE, TK Elevator, Mitsubishi Electric. What each discloses about the split between new equipment and service, how many units each maintains, the margin gap between the two businesses, and by what mechanism the OEMs hold the service annuity. Every figure below carries its basis label: `Disclosed` (from the filing/report itself) · `Press-derived` · `Estimated`. Where a number is computed, the arithmetic is shown.

**Headline structural change during this pass.** On 29 April 2026 KONE agreed to acquire TK Elevator. Five global OEMs are on a path to four. This is dated and sourced in §D below and it changes the answer to Part C of the brief.

#### The margin gap — the whole strategic story, with the arithmetic

- FY2025: Service 25.1% − New Equipment 4.8% = **20.3 percentage points**.
- As a multiple: 25.1 ÷ 4.8 = **5.23×**. Service earns 5.2 turns of margin for every one turn New Equipment earns.
- FY2024: 24.6% − 6.1% = 18.5 percentage points. So the gap **widened by 1.8 points in one year** (20.3 − 18.5), because New Equipment margin fell 1.3 points while Service margin rose 0.5.
- Most recent quarter available (Q2 2026, released July 2026): New Equipment $1,279M / $40M OP / **3.1%**; Service $2,580M / $599M OP / **23.2%**. Gap = 23.2 − 3.1 = **20.1 points**; ratio 23.2 ÷ 3.1 = **7.48×**. Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/otis-reports-second-quarter-2026-results-302832034.html (Basis: Disclosed)

#### Revenue mix and profit mix — quoted from the 10-K

The 10-K states New Equipment and Service "contributed 35% and 65% of our net sales, and 9% and 91% of our segment operating profit, respectively" for 2025. Verifying:
- Sales: 4,989 ÷ 14,431 = 34.57% → 35% ✓; 9,442 ÷ 14,431 = 65.43% → 65% ✓
- Segment operating profit: total = 240 + 2,374 = 2,614. New Equipment 240 ÷ 2,614 = **9.18%** → 9% ✓; Service 2,374 ÷ 2,614 = **90.82%** → 91% ✓

**Read that again: 35% of the revenue produces 91% of the profit.** New equipment is, on Otis's own numbers, close to a customer-acquisition cost for the service annuity.

#### Maintained units — the numerator

> "We have a maintenance portfolio of approximately 2.5 million units globally, which includes Otis equipment manufactured and sold by us, as well as equipment from other original equipment manufacturers." — Form 10-K FY2025, Item 1 (Basis: **Disclosed**)

> "For the fourth consecutive year, we grew our maintenance portfolio 4%, ending 2025 with an industry-leading approximately 2.5 million units under contract." — Otis Annual Report 2025, p.3, "Service growth strengthened by portfolio expansion" (Basis: **Disclosed**)

- Portfolio growth: **+4% units in 2025**, and 4% in each of the three prior years. (Disclosed)
- Connected units: "approximately 1.1 million units of our global portfolio…are connected" via Otis ONE at year-end 2025. Computed penetration: 1.1 ÷ 2.5 = **44%** of the portfolio is on Otis's own IoT platform. (Units Disclosed; percentage Estimated by division)
- Derived revenue per maintained unit, **global**: Service $9,442M ÷ 2.5M units = **$3,777 per unit per year**; maintenance-and-repair only $7,584M ÷ 2.5M = **$3,034 per unit per year**. (Basis: Estimated — computed from two disclosed figures, and the 2.5M is itself "approximately")

#### Geography — and the warning that goes with it

|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | **4,192** | 4,239 | 4,030 | Disclosed |

- US share of Otis revenue: 4,192 ÷ 14,431 = **29.05%**. Consistent with the 10-K's "Our international operations represented approximately 71% of our net sales for 2025." (100 − 71 = 29 ✓)
- US revenue **fell** year over year: 4,192 − 4,239 = −$47M, = −1.1%.
- **Otis does not disclose a US-only or Americas-only maintained-unit count, and does not disclose segment operating profit by geography.** The 2.5 million units is a global figure. Do not apply it to the US installed base without saying so.

#### Otis's own statement about independents — the single most useful sentence in this pass

> "In the Service segment, independent service providers and other small operators are significant competitors in most of our local geographies. These independent service providers have an aggregate portfolio of about 50% of service units, but account for a smaller percentage of the service business when measured by value." — Form 10-K FY2025, Item 1, Competition (Basis: **Disclosed**)

Two things follow, and they are the commercial thesis of this whole industry for a buy-side practice:
1. **The OEMs do not hold the service base. Roughly half of it sits with independents** — on the market leader's own disclosure, in its own SEC filing.
2. That half is the **low-revenue-per-unit half**. "A smaller percentage of the service business when measured by value" means the independent-held units bill less per unit than OEM-held units. That is simultaneously the reason the OEMs have not bothered to take them and the reason a consolidator can buy them cheaply and reprice them.

Note: Otis states its portfolio "includes…equipment from other original equipment manufacturers" — Otis services rival-brand units. So the lock-in described in §B is asymmetric and imperfect, not absolute.

## B. THE LOCK-IN MECHANISM

### B1. The hardest evidence: a federal appellate opinion that describes the mechanism in detail — and then blesses it

**In re Elevator Antitrust Litigation, United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, Docket No. 06-3128-cv, decided 4 September 2007** (reported at 502 F.3d 47). Panel: Jacobs, C.J., Straub and B.D. Parker, Circ. JJ. Defendants: United Technologies Corporation and Otis Elevator Company; Kone Corporation and Kone, Inc.; Schindler Holding Ltd. and Schindler Elevator Corporation; ThyssenKrupp AG, ThyssenKrupp Elevator Corporation and ThyssenKrupp Elevator Capital Corporation.
Source: https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-2nd-circuit/1069452.html

The plaintiffs alleged each defendant engaged in exclusionary conduct including:

> "designing the elevators to prevent servicing by other providers (including each other); refusing to sell competitors the parts, tools, software or diagrams necessary to service the elevators"

and that defendants created

> "proprietary maintenance tools which are not made available to competing service companies (e.g., embedded computer systems that can only be interfaced with defendant-controlled handheld units)."

**The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal.** Its reasoning on the refusal-to-deal claim:

> "the complaint does not allege that defendants terminated a prior relationship with elevator service providers—a change which (by taking advantage of their customers' sunk costs) could evince monopolistic motives."

(Basis: **Disclosed** — court opinion.)

**Why this is the single most important document in the pass.** It does two things at once. First, it puts the mechanism on the record in a federal appellate opinion, in the OEMs' own aftermarket, in precise terms: *embedded computer systems that can only be interfaced with manufacturer-controlled handheld units*. Second, and more consequentially for a buy-side practice, **it holds that this is lawful in the United States absent a terminated prior course of dealing.** Under *Trinko*/*Aspen Skiing* doctrine as applied here, a manufacturer that has never sold tools to independents has no antitrust duty to start. The lock-in is therefore not a legal grey area that a plaintiff or a regulator is about to sweep away. It is a durable structural feature of the US market. Nineteen years on, no successor US case has disturbed it — searches for post-2020 US elevator monopolization or aftermarket class actions returned nothing.

### B2. The European precedent, dated precisely

**Commission Decision of 21 February 2007, Case COMP/E-1/38.823 — Elevators and Escalators.**

The Court of Justice's own press release describes it as cartels

> "on the market for the sale, installation, maintenance and modernisation of elevators and escalators"

in **Belgium, Germany, Luxembourg and the Netherlands**, with

> "the Commission imposed fines totalling more than €992 million on Otis, Kone, Schindler and Thyssenkrupp"

Source (Court of Justice of the EU press release): https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2012-06/cp120088en.pdf (Basis: **Disclosed**)
Case page: https://competition-cases.ec.europa.eu/cases/AT.38823 · Commission press release IP/07/209: https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_07_209 (both are JavaScript-rendered and the substantive text could not be extracted in this pass)
Cartel period reported as **1995 to 2004**: https://www.hausfeld.com/en-gb/what-we-do/current-claims/elevators-escalators-cartel (Basis: **Press-derived**)

**The analytically important detail is the word "maintenance" in the scope.** This was not merely a new-installation bid-rigging cartel. The maintenance and modernisation of the installed base was inside the cartelised conduct. Hausfeld also notes "the cartelised installation resulted in a market distortion on the maintenance market as well" — i.e. rigging who installed the unit determined who owned the annuity. (Basis: Press-derived)

**Individual fine amounts per company: NOT verified in this pass. Left empty.** Multiple secondary sources circulate a breakdown but none of the primary documents I could render carried the table. Do not publish per-company fine figures until the OJ summary (CELEX 52008XC0326(01), https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52008XC0326(01)) or the decision PDF (https://ec.europa.eu/competition/antitrust/cases/dec_docs/38823/38823_1340_4.pdf) is retrieved — both were rate-limited/unavailable during this pass.

### B3. The Spanish thread — a competition authority pursuing the *obstruction of independents* specifically

This is the closest any regulator has come to attacking the lock-in itself rather than price-fixing.

- **8 May 2012** — Spain's Comisión Nacional de la Competencia (CNC) opened enforcement proceedings against **Zardoya Otis and Schindler** for "obstaculizar supuestamente la actividad de los competidores" — allegedly obstructing the activity of competitors — in the elevator maintenance and repair sector, via "engañoso y denigratorio" (misleading and denigratory) messaging to customers. Source: https://facua.org/noticias/zardoya-otis-y-schindler-expedientadas-por-obstaculizar-a-sus-competidores-en-el-sector-de-los-ascensores/ (Basis: **Press-derived**; the article body is partly paywalled and the expediente number was not visible)
- **September 2013** — the CNC fined **four elevator manufacturers €4.8 million** for discrediting competitors. Sources: https://www.eleconomista.es/economia/noticias/5164476/09/13/Economia-Empresas-La-CNC-multa-con-48-millones-a-cuatro-fabricantes-de-ascensores-por-desacreditar-a-competidores.html (returned HTTP 403 on fetch; headline only) and https://www.law360.com/articles/474839/spain-fines-elevator-repair-cos-4-8m-in-antitrust-probe (paywalled). Related CNMC resolutions located but not read: **S/0403/12 (10 April 2013)** https://vlex.es/vid/433694510 and **S/DC/0502/14 (30 October 2014)** https://vlex.es/vid/588696378. (Basis: **Press-derived**, headline-level only)

**The named companies, the fine amount and the conduct description here should be treated as press-derived and unconfirmed until the CNMC resolutions themselves are read.** The pattern — an OEM telling a building owner that only the OEM can safely or lawfully maintain its equipment — is the exact commercial expression of the technical lock-in, and it is worth running to primary source in a later pass.

### B4. What actually happens at the boundary — can an independent take over an OEM unit?

Answer: **it depends almost entirely on the vintage and the controller**, and the trade literature is consistent on the shape of it even though it is not filing-grade evidence.

- Proprietary controllers "may restrict access to software, service tools, diagnostic functions, or replacement parts." — https://www.cedelevator.com/controller-modernization-sourcing-non-proprietary-systems/ (Basis: Press-derived, trade)
- "When an OEM installs elevator proprietary controllers, drives, or software, they control the entire ecosystem." OEMs also declare equipment obsolete early — equipment "maybe as young as five to seven years old" — and "many elevator contracts include exclusions for obsolete elevator parts, giving the elevator service provider an exit from coverage obligations." — The Elevator Consultant, https://theelevatorconsultant.com/the-double-standard-in-elevator-maintenance-contracts-when-oems-wont-cover-their-own-equipment/ (Basis: Press-derived, consultant)
- A vintage threshold is asserted: equipment "15+ years old" typically uses "open-architecture systems that any qualified technician can service," while equipment "installed after 2010" likely requires OEM diagnostics; and specifically "Otis Gen3 and Gen360 systems, KONE EcoSpace drives, and other modern equipment require proprietary diagnostic tools that OEMs legally protect." Independents are said to price 20–30% below OEMs, but "the cost advantage disappears" where diagnostic lock-in applies. — Elevator Blueprint, published 26 March 2026, updated 2 June 2026, https://elevatorblueprint.com/blog/oem-vs-independent-elevator-company/ (Basis: **Press-derived**, trade blog — the vintage threshold and the named product families are NOT independently verified and should not be repeated as fact without checking)
- The parts channel, from a former industry operator: "Who controls the parts market? We've sat in on meetings in regard to how do we slow the independence down? You slow the independence down by slowing down the parts." — Ashleigh Wilson, founder of AuditMate, https://proptechvc.com/podcast/the-problem-with-the-big-4-in-the-elevator-industry-why-independents-cant-break-into-the-industry (Basis: **Press-derived**, first-person industry account, undated)
- Non-proprietary controller suppliers exist and are the practical escape route: **SmartRise** ("open-market controllers with universal boards for hydraulic and traction systems") and **Virginia Controls** ("non-proprietary software and industrial-grade components"), plus **Motion Control Engineering (MCE)**, now Nidec Elevator Group (https://acim.nidec.com/elevators/motion-control-engineering/about-mce). (Basis: Press-derived, vendor material)
- Contractual friction sits on top of the technical friction: OEM maintenance agreements typically require cancellation notice "90 days, no more than 120 days by certified mail," described as "extremely hard to cancel." (AuditMate, as above; Basis: Press-derived)

**The cost of escaping the lock-in was not verified.** No reliable, sourced dollar figure for replacing a proprietary controller with a non-proprietary one was obtained in this pass. **Left empty.**

**And a live regulatory negative.** US right-to-repair legislation — which now exists in some form in a majority of states and covers consumer electronics, agricultural equipment, wheelchairs and automobiles — **does not cover elevators**. Searches across state right-to-repair trackers (https://www.repair.org/legislation and state-by-state compilations) returned no elevator-specific right-to-repair statute or bill. Nor is there an elevator equivalent of the FTC's 2026 John Deere software-access order. (Basis: **Estimated** — this is a negative finding from absence of search results, not a positive disclosure; it should be confirmed with a legislative database before being relied on.)

**The one countervailing code hook** — ASME A17.1/CSA B44 Section 8.6 requires a written **Maintenance Control Program** to be maintained and available on site, which in principle forces documentation of maintenance procedures for whoever holds the contract. The specific text on special tools and software access could not be retrieved (up.codes is disallowed by robots.txt). Relevant secondary references: https://elevatorworld.com/article/maintenance-control-program-changes/ · https://www.elevatorinfo.org/elevator-maintenance-control-programs-mcp-building-owners-managers/ · Illinois State Fire Marshal MCP guidance https://sfm.illinois.gov/content/dam/soi/en/web/sfm/sfmdocuments/documents/maintenancecontrolprogram.pdf. **Not verified. Flagged for a follow-up pass — this is potentially the strongest pro-independent legal lever in the US and it deserves a proper read of A17.1 §8.6.**

### B5. The modern layer: connectivity as the new lock-in

Otis discloses that **~1.1 million of its ~2.5 million maintained units are connected** to Otis ONE, its cloud IoT platform (10-K FY2025). Computed penetration **44%**. Otis's Q4 2025 commentary reports **subscription revenue up 35% in 2025** (https://stockstory.org/us/stocks/nyse/otis/news/earnings-call/otis-q4-deep-dive-service-segment-momentum-and-modernization-backlog-shape-2026-outlook, Basis: Press-derived).

This is the mechanism's second generation. The first generation was a handheld tool you could not buy. The second is a telemetry stream and a monitoring contract that the building owner has come to rely on, which travels with the OEM and not with the unit. An independent taking over a connected unit does not inherit the data feed.

### B6. Where the OEMs are structurally weak — the opening

This is where the pass earns its keep, because two of the three pieces of evidence are OEM self-disclosure.

1. **Otis, in its 10-K, concedes half the units.** "These independent service providers have an aggregate portfolio of about 50% of service units, but account for a smaller percentage of the service business when measured by value." The OEMs have chosen the high-value-per-unit half. Otis's global revenue per maintained unit computes to **$3,777/yr**; TKE publishes **~EUR 3.1k/yr**; KONE computes to **EUR 2,641/yr**. The independent-held half sits below these.
2. **Schindler is actively walking away from Americas service work.** CFO Carla De Geyseleer, on the FY2025 call: "In Americas, we saw a modest decrease…result of our **increased selectivity when it comes to recaptures**." A "recapture" is winning a unit back from whoever currently services it — usually an independent. Schindler is declining recaptures in the Americas on economic grounds. CEO Paolo Compagna, same call: the Americas will show "**the lowest growth rate**" of any region in service. An OEM saying, on the record, that it is being choosier about US route work is the clearest possible statement of where the OEM economic model runs out.
3. **A material minority of the OEMs' own new installations never enter their own portfolio.** TKE discloses "**~70% NI conversion into UuM**" — so roughly 30% of TKE's own new units go to somebody else on day one. Independent analysis puts global conversion at "2/3 of elevators installed convert to a service agreement once the initial service period wears out," with ">80% in Europe" and ~25% in China (https://oliversung.substack.com/p/elevator-companies, 10 April 2023, Basis: Press-derived/Estimated). The lock-in is real but it is not hermetic even on the OEM's own iron.
4. **Retention is high but not total.** TKE publishes ">90% Service retention" (Disclosed). Schindler's CEO claims "industry-leading retention rates" without a number. Otis publishes portfolio *growth* (+4%/yr, four years running) but **no retention or attrition rate**. A >90% retention rate implies attrition of up to ~10% per year — on a 1.4m-unit base that is up to 140,000 units per year in motion at TKE alone. **Note: none of the five discloses a gross attrition figure. All "retention" numbers here are IR-presentation figures, not audited.**

**What the OEMs are NOT doing, stated plainly:** they are not buying, and on Schindler's own testimony are selectively declining, the geographically dispersed, low-billing, low-density half of the service base. That half is roughly 50% of units globally on Otis's disclosure. It is the inventory a route consolidator buys.

## C. OEM ACQUISITION BEHAVIOUR — do they buy independent routes?

**Yes. All four Western OEMs buy independent service books, and they do so specifically to add maintenance units and route density.** This is dated, sourced and unambiguous. It means the OEMs are **both a competing bidder and an exit path**.
