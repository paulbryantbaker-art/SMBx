## W1 · WED — Teardown, carousel [Q03] · P1

**Caption:**

Think about the last deal you lost after signing the LOI.

Was it the money? Or was it something you found in week seven that was sitting there in week one?

The odds say the second one. Axial's work on broken LOIs puts non-QoE diligence findings at 25.3% of post-exclusivity deaths and QoE EBITDA discrepancies at another 21.3%. Roughly 47% between them.

Financing? Down to 10.7%, from 21.3% two years earlier.

Which means the constraint on your pipeline has moved and your process may not have. If your diligence sequence was built when financing was the thing that killed deals, you are front-loading the wrong questions and discovering the expensive ones on somebody else's clock.

Two things I'd want you to know before you use that number.

It's self-reported by members of a deal network — buyers and advisors who use that channel and were willing to describe a loss. That's a real sampling limit and I'd rather hand it to you than let the statistic do more work than it can carry.

And it does not say that looking earlier prevents any of this. Nobody has published that study. I believe it from twenty years of watching where deals die, but belief isn't evidence and I'm not going to sell it to you as one.

The money is not your constraint anymore. The looking is.

## W1 · FRI — Practitioner note · P3

If you're holding a 2021 vintage, you already know the exit multiple isn't coming to rescue it.

So the question on your desk is the one that used to be somebody else's problem: where does the return actually come from now?

In the trades, my experience is that it comes from four places and only four. Pricing discipline. Dispatch and technician utilization. Attachment of service agreements. And the back office you stop paying for twice.

Everything else in the synergy page is decoration.

Here's the part that matters for what you're doing this month: none of those four are post-close problems. Every one of them is visible in diligence — if you go looking for them instead of looking at them.

Has this business ever raised price and survived it? Is dispatch a system, or is it one person's memory? Is the agreement base real, or is it a folder of expired PDFs somebody counted once?

I've sat in deals where the model assumed all four and the diligence tested none. The number in the model didn't move. The business did.

If operations have to carry the return, operations have to be underwritten before you sign — not planned after you close.

## W2 · MON — Market map, carousel [Q20] · P2

**Caption:**

You've probably been told Dallas–Fort Worth home services is picked over. Four big consolidators, market's gone, you're late.

Somebody told me the same thing. It's eighteen.

Not eighteen companies — eighteen distinct platform owners operating in this metro, which is the number that actually affects you. Some you'd name immediately. Most you wouldn't, because they trade under brands that look like independent local operators and have for years.

Two things that changes for you.

Your competitive set is four times what you were told, and roughly two-thirds of it is invisible at the brand layer. You are going to bid against people you didn't know were in the market, and you'll find out at the LOI stage, which is the expensive place to find out.

And the target you're circling may already be owned. Not owned in a way you'd see on a website — owned in a way you'd see in a filing, if anyone had looked.

"Saturated" is what a market feels like when nobody has done the corporate development work on it. In DFW specifically that feeling is off by a factor of four.

One metro, one vertical, built from license registries and company sources rather than a tracker. If you're buying here and want to know where your name sits on it, ask.

## W2 · WED — How buyers think [Q04] · P1

How long does a deal stay alive in your shop after the diligence has already told you no?

Family offices average 37 days under exclusivity before they walk. Funds take 106.

Same disappointing finding. Roughly three times the runway before somebody says it out loud.

I don't think that gap is about conviction. It's about who has to be told.

A principal can kill a deal in a hallway. No IC calendar, no partner who championed it in March, no pacing pressure making a dead deal look alive for another quarter. The cost of stopping is one conversation.

Inside a fund, stopping costs a deal team the thing they've been presenting since spring. So the deal doesn't die — it goes quiet, gets restructured, gets renegotiated, and dies anyway on day 106 with another $200,000 of third-party spend attached.

Neither is a character flaw. It's structure, and it's worth knowing which one you're inside.

Because the expensive part isn't the broken fees. It's that your two deal people spent sixty days on a file that had already answered the question — and whatever they didn't source in those sixty days is the hole in next quarter's pipeline.

Figures from Axial's broken-LOI work, self-reported by users of a deal network. That's the limitation to hold onto.

## W2 · FRI — Practitioner note, carousel · P3

Your integration playbook has five hundred questions in it and you have never once finished it.

Nobody has. In a lower-middle-market deal the five-hundred-question version is a consulting product, not an integration — and the honest result is that it gets abandoned in week two and you integrate on instinct anyway.

The version that works is about twenty. Here's the list.

Every one of them is answerable before you close. Each one you skip gets answered in month four, when fixing it costs money and somebody has already quit over it.

I'll be straight about what this is: the twenty questions are the diagnosis, not the treatment. Knowing which ones matter is maybe a third of the work. What you do when question eleven comes back wrong is the rest, and that doesn't fit on a slide.

But the twenty are yours. Take them into your next one.

**Slides:**

1. Five hundred questions is a consulting product. Twenty is an integration.
2. Who actually makes the schedule tomorrow morning?
3. Is dispatch a system, or one person's memory?
4. Has this business ever raised price and survived it?
5. What share of revenue is under agreement — and are the agreements current?
6. Which customers only stay because of one named person?
7. What does the owner personally still do every day?
8. Who reports to the owner that shouldn't?
9. What's on the trucks, and who decides?
10. Which systems do we keep, and who says so?
11. What's the first invoice we send under our name, and when?
12. What are we telling the technicians, and who tells them?
13. What do we stop paying for on day one?
14. What breaks if the owner leaves in ninety days?
15. Answer them before you close. Or answer them in month four, expensively.

## W3 · MON — How buyers think [Q06 · Q07 · Q08] · P4

You're deciding whether to hire a business development person. Here's the number the conversation usually leaves out.

The hire is $150,000 to $250,000 all-in before they have sourced anything.

Then the stack they need. Deal databases, contact data, CRM, whatever license reconstructs the private-company universe in your vertical. Overlapping subscriptions clear $100,000 a year without trying, and most of the overlap stays invisible until somebody audits it.

Call it a quarter-million to three hundred thousand before the first proprietary conversation happens.

That is not an argument against hiring. Plenty of acquirers should, and if you're doing four deals a year you almost certainly should. It's an argument for making the comparison against the real number — because in every one of these conversations I've sat in, the comparison gets made against the salary alone, never the salary plus the stack plus the ramp.

Two gaps you should know about before you use any of this.

The most-quoted statistic on how many funds have nobody doing origination at all is from 2021, and the vendor stopped publishing it. I won't hand you a four-year-old number as if it describes your market today.

And the figure that would actually settle your decision — how long from hiring a BD professional to their first sourced, closed deal — isn't published by anyone. Not the funds, not the recruiters, not the people selling you the alternative. I've looked.

When the deciding number doesn't exist, you're making a judgment call, not running a calculation. Worth knowing which one you're doing.

## W3 · WED — Practitioner note [Q18] · P2

Your target list says independent.

Are you sure? Or does it say "we didn't find an owner"?

Those are different sentences and only one of them is a finding. On my register, independent means exactly one thing: not matched to a known owner. That is a statement about the limits of my matching, not a statement about the company.

Here's what that looks like in practice. In one vertical I work, six companies trade under the same name. Not similar — the same one. Different states, different owners, no relationship between them, and at least one already sitting inside a platform. I found the sixth by accident, weeks after I'd built the board and called it finished.

That failure doesn't announce itself. Your list has a company on it, the company is real, the name matches — and you've either merged two businesses into one row or dropped a live target because the name collided with something you screened out in March.

The board just quietly becomes wrong in a way that reads as complete. Which is the only kind of wrong that survives a partner meeting.

So when a list reaches you with "independent" in a column, the question worth asking the person who built it is: does that mean unowned, or does it mean unmatched?

If they can't tell you, it means unmatched.

## W3 · FRI — Broker-facing · P1

If you run sell-side processes, you can tell inside four minutes which buyers are real.

I've been on the buy-side of a lot of your processes, and I don't think it's the number that tells you. Here's what I think it is.

They read the materials. All of them, before the first call. The questions they ask are the questions of somebody who did the reading, and that's audible immediately.

They tell you what they can't do. Financing structure, timeline, board approvals — early. A buyer who surfaces their own constraint on day three is worth more to your process than one who discovers it on day fifty.

Their diligence list is shaped like a thesis. If it's a generic 500-item pull with nothing specific to this business on it, nobody on that side has decided why they want it.

And they don't retrade on things they could have found before the LOI. Some retrades are legitimate — the business changed, or something was concealed. Most are a buyer discovering in week eight what was available in week one and asking your client to pay for it.

That last one is the whole thing. A buyer who front-loads the ugly questions is a buyer whose LOI means something when you take it to your seller.

I'm buy-side only. I'm not competing for your mandate — I'd just rather my clients be the ones you're glad picked up the phone.

## W4 · MON — Teardown, carousel [Q14] · P3

**Caption:**

Your model exits at 10x. What you're buying today trades at 5.9x.

That gradient — $10M–$25M of enterprise value at roughly 5.9x, $100M–$250M at roughly 10.0x — is the entire lower-middle-market thesis. It's also the number I see underwritten most carelessly.

Because the four turns aren't paid for assembling revenue. They're paid for a platform that got bigger *and* better: one management team, one system, real density in a market, margins that survived the integration.

The other version is the one you don't want to be holding at exit. Eight acquisitions, eight sets of books, eight dispatch systems, eight founders still running their own shops under a shared logo. That platform isn't a 10x business. It's a 5.9x business with more headaches, and the buyer's diligence will find it in about a week.

So the question your IC should be asking on the next add-on isn't whether it's accretive. It's whether it's actually being integrated, or just consolidated onto a cap table.

The gradient describes what happens when integration works. It does not describe what happens when buying happens.

Figures are GF Data — sponsored deals only, reported by participants. Directionally solid, not a census.

## W4 · WED — Practitioner note [Q22] · P2

The market study on your desk has a source on every figure. That is not the same as being right, and the difference has cost people real money.

Here's how I know. I ran a traceability check on one of my own: 386 of 386 figures sourced. Clean.

Three days later, three claim clusters in that same document failed primary-source verification.

An all-sector buyout total that had been relabeled somewhere upstream as sector-specific. A market size attributed to a Census table it does not appear in. A recurring-revenue percentage with no origin at all — it had simply been in the material long enough to read as established.

Every one traced perfectly. A faithfully carried mistake audits green.

So when a study reaches your IC, the check that matters isn't "does this number have a source." It's two harder questions: does the source say what we say it says, and was the source in a position to know?

The second one is where vendor research usually falls down — not because anyone lied, but because the firm that published the market size sells into that market.

That check is slower and less satisfying and it's the whole job. There is a shorter version of this work that skips it. It produces documents that look identical to yours until somebody in a partner meeting asks where the number came from.

## W4 · FRI — Practitioner note · P3

You close on Friday. Your best technician has three competitors' numbers in his phone and he's spent the week hearing the owner sold.

The first ninety days decide the next three years, and not for strategic reasons. Because of who quits.

In a tight trade labor market your top two can be gone in a fortnight, and they leave with the customers who only stayed for them. That's not an HR problem. That's your revenue model.

What I've seen work, and none of it is complicated:

Somebody from your side is physically there in week one. Not a video call. There.

The seller stays visible for a defined period and says out loud that they chose you. An owner who disappears at close tells the crew something you cannot afterward untell.

Nothing changes on the trucks or the schedule for the first month unless it's actively on fire. Every change you make in week two is a change made before you understood why it was that way.

And the money conversation happens early — comp, benefits, whether the pay structure survives. If you don't answer it, the parking lot will.

The strategic work is real and it comes second. Month one is about the crew deciding whether this was a good thing that happened to them. Once they've decided it wasn't, you don't get to re-decide it.

Twenty years in and around trades integration, and this is the part I'd defend hardest: the deal thesis does not survive the people leaving.
