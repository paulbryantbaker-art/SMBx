# THE U.S. COMMERCIAL MECHANICAL, HVAC & PLUMBING SERVICES MARKET
## A Buy-Side Market Assessment for Private Equity and Institutional Acquirers

**Prepared by smbX.ai — Buy-Side Corporate Development**
**July 2026 | Scope: Commercial / Nonresidential Only**

*This report combines proprietary buy-side research with published industry, government, and market-research sources. Where analyst estimates disagree, both figures are presented with attribution; discrepancies are noted plainly rather than reconciled. All triangulations and practitioner estimates are flagged as such. A data-discrepancy register appears in Appendix B.*

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The commercial mechanical, HVAC, and plumbing services sector is undergoing a structural transformation from a cyclical, project-oriented construction trade into an institutionalized, mission-critical infrastructure asset class. Three forces are converging to produce what may be the most attractive buy-and-build environment in the trades: an unprecedented demand shock from hyperscale data-center construction, an accelerating but still-early consolidation cycle, and a persistent, quantifiable valuation arbitrage between project-led and service-led business models.

The market is large by any measure, though analysts size it differently depending on scope. Comfort Systems USA's FY2025 10-K estimates U.S. commercial, industrial, and institutional mechanical and electrical contracting at approximately $700 billion — up from $550 billion in its FY2024 filing and $350 billion in FY2023, a series whose rapid escalation itself signals the data-center re-rating. Stripping the electrical bundle using Comfort Systems' own segment mix implies a commercial-mechanical-only market of roughly $450–520 billion including new construction, with the recurring aftermarket service core — the segment private equity actually targets — in the $25–45 billion range and growing mid-single digits.

Consolidation is real, accelerating, and unfinished. Capstone Partners counted 149 HVAC transactions in 2025, up 12.9% year over year, in what it calls a "widely fragmented market." S&P Global Market Intelligence reported private equity add-on activity in HVAC services up 88% year over year through mid-2025, while noting that residential HVAC is roughly midway through its consolidation cycle and commercial HVAC services remain "still in the early stages." Platforms have proven the model — Service Logic scaled past $2.2 billion in revenue before its December 2025 sale to Bain Capital and Mubadala; Crete United went from zero to roughly $1 billion in four years; PremiStar grew revenue and EBITDA more than six-fold in five years — yet no platform holds meaningful national share of a 120,000-contractor universe.

The central value-creation mechanism is the service-mix arbitrage. Project-heavy plan-spec contractors transact at the low end of the multiple spectrum, while operators with 40%+ of gross profit from preventive maintenance agreements command premiums of one to five turns depending on the source consulted — different analysts report materially different tier structures, and this report presents both frameworks side by side in Section 8. What no source disputes is the direction: recurring commercial service revenue re-rates the asset, generates proprietary pull-through work, and insulates earnings from construction cyclicality.

The risk profile is the moat. Percentage-of-completion accounting, WIP schedule integrity, working-capital pegs, surety and bonding transfer, customer concentration, and — for union signatories — multiemployer pension withdrawal liability make commercial diligence materially harder than residential. The U.S. Supreme Court's May 2026 decision in *M&K Employee Solutions v. Trustees of the IAM National Pension Fund* underscored how consequential the pension dimension has become. This complexity suppresses competition for targets and rewards buyers with genuine corporate-development capability. In commercial mechanical M&A, structure matters more than headline multiple.

The primary caution is concentration: much of the sector's re-rating traces to a single end market — data centers — whose capital-expenditure cycle has never been tested by a downturn at anything approaching current scale.

## SECTION 1 — MARKET DEFINITION AND SCOPE DISCIPLINE

This report covers commercial and nonresidential mechanical services only: commercial HVAC installation and service, mechanical construction and pipefitting, commercial and industrial plumbing, building automation and controls, and the boundary trades (fire protection, industrial refrigeration) where they sit inside mechanical platforms. End markets include commercial office and retail, healthcare, education, government and municipal, industrial and manufacturing, life sciences and semiconductor, and data centers and mission-critical facilities.

Residential home services — including the residential consolidators Apex Service Partners (now $3B+ revenue per its May 2026 disclosure), Wrench Group, Sila Services, and Champions Group — are outside the scope of this report and appear only where a residential benchmark illuminates commercial pricing.

A note on data hygiene that governs everything that follows: the most commonly cited headline figures for this industry blend residential with commercial revenue, or bundle mechanical with electrical contracting. IBISWorld's $159.4 billion figure for Heating & Air-Conditioning Contractors (2026) is residential and commercial combined. Comfort Systems' ~$700 billion figure bundles electrical. Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational data cannot separate commercial from residential technicians. Every figure in this report states its scope, and every commercial-only derivation states its split assumption. Where the underlying sources genuinely disagree, both figures are cited and the disagreement is stated plainly.

## SECTION 2 — MARKET SIZING: TAM, SAM, AND SOM

### 2.1 Total Addressable Market

There is no single authoritative commercial-only market figure. The honest presentation is a set of concentric frames, each tied to its source, with analyst disagreements noted.

**Frame 1 — Commercial M&E contracting (broadest).** Comfort Systems USA's FY2025 Form 10-K estimates that commercial, industrial, and institutional mechanical and electrical contracting generates approximately **$700 billion** in annual U.S. revenue. The same estimate was approximately $550 billion in the FY2024 10-K, $350 billion in FY2023, and $100 billion as recently as FY2019. The roughly seven-fold rise over six years reflects methodology changes, inflation, and the data-center capex boom as much as organic market growth; the series should be treated as directional. This frame bundles electrical contracting and overstates the mechanical-only opportunity.

**Frame 2 — Commercial mechanical-only (headline).** Applying Comfort Systems' own FY2025 revenue mix (approximately 73% mechanical, 27% electrical) to the M&E frame yields a commercial mechanical contracting market — HVAC, plumbing, piping, controls, fire protection; installation plus service — of roughly **$450–520 billion**. This is a triangulation, flagged as such, and it is the headline commercial-mechanical TAM used in this report.

**Frame 3 — The recurring aftermarket service core.** The commercial HVAC/mechanical services aftermarket (maintenance, repair, replacement — the recurring revenue pool that platforms are actually built on) is the hardest slice to size, and analysts report it very differently:

| Source | Figure | Scope note |
|---|---|---|
| One industry construct (as compiled in prior MEP research) | U.S. HVAC contractor services ~$152B, of which commercial service & repair ~$33.4B (22%) and commercial new install ~$18.2B (12%) | Primary attribution for the splits not located; presented as reported |
| Mordor Intelligence (Jan 2026 vintage) | U.S. HVAC services $17.93B (2025) → $25.35B (2031), 5.9% CAGR | All end-uses; commercial is a subset. Mordor's own publications also show $21.16B (2025) → $29.13B (2030) at 6.6%, and $28.2B → $38.8B — the same firm's pages disagree |
| Grand View Research | North America HVAC services $39.41B (2024), 4.3% CAGR to 2030 | All end-uses, North America |

Different analysts report differently; the definitional gaps (what counts as "services," which end-uses, equipment vs. labor) explain most but not all of the spread. Our synthesis, flagged as a triangulation: the U.S. **commercial mechanical aftermarket services core is roughly $25–45 billion** and compounding mid-single digits.

**Commercial plumbing.** Sources again diverge. Prior MEP research cites a U.S. total plumbing industry of $191.4 billion by 2026 with commercial/nonresidential/municipal work at more than two-thirds of revenue, implying a commercial plumbing TAM of approximately **$127 billion**; a primary source for the $191.4 billion figure could not be located, and it sits awkwardly beside IBISWorld's $159.4 billion for the combined heating-and-air segment. A Census-basis aggregation of full NAICS 238220 (plumbing, heating, and air-conditioning contractors together) runs far larger (~$392 billion across ~111,000 establishments per one Census-citing aggregator). The scopes are incompatible — the broader plumbing figures sweep in municipal, infrastructure, and utility-line work outside the contractor codes. Both are presented here; readers should treat the commercial plumbing TAM as somewhere between a NAICS-disciplined slice and the ~$127 billion broad-scope figure, and we note plainly that different analysts report differently. What is well-sourced is the demand foundation: the EPA's 7th Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey (September 2023) identifies **$625 billion of drinking-water infrastructure need over 20 years** — a 32% increase over the prior survey — with a further ~$630 billion of wastewater/stormwater need per the 2022 Clean Watersheds Needs Survey.

**Supporting context.** Private nonresidential construction spending runs at roughly a $1.25 trillion seasonally adjusted annual rate (Census, spring 2026). MEP systems typically account for an estimated 25–45% of commercial facility construction cost — low end for office and retail, high end for hospitals, labs, and data centers (practitioner estimate). North America commercial facility maintenance was ~$153.6 billion in 2023, growing ~4.1% annually toward ~$202 billion by 2030, with hard services (HVAC, electrical, plumbing, fire) roughly 35% of the U.S. facility-management market (Grand View Research; figures pending primary verification).

### 2.2 Serviceable Addressable Market

The PE-acquirable universe is privately held, commercial-focused mechanical, HVAC, and plumbing contractors of roughly $2–250 million in revenue. The fragmentation evidence: IBISWorld counts **120,461 U.S. HVAC contractor businesses in 2026** (residential and commercial combined, +1.7% year over year, "low market share concentration" — the largest player, EMCOR, holds only low-single-digit share). Roughly 70% of establishments have fewer than ten employees and are too small to platform. The commercial-capable subset is plausibly 20,000–40,000 firms, of which perhaps **3,000–6,000 clear the ~$5 million-plus revenue, institutional-quality bar** that platforms like FirstCall Mechanical (which publicly targets "$5M+ in annual revenue") and PremiStar actually acquire. These are triangulations, flagged as such.

**SAM estimate: roughly $120–200 billion of revenue** held by privately owned commercial mechanical contractors in the acquirable band — the addressable pool for buy-and-build.

### 2.3 Serviceable Obtainable Market

Benchmarked against what real platforms achieved, a disciplined new commercial platform with committed capital and a credible operator can realistically assemble **$150–500 million of revenue over a four-to-six-year hold** via a founder platform plus eight to twenty add-ons — the Crete United, FirstCall, and Pueblo band. Reaching $1 billion (Crete United's four-year trajectory) is achievable with aggressive deployment in a supportive M&A market. Even at the top of that range the platform holds well under half a percent of the commercial-mechanical TAM. The binding constraints are sourcing at reasonable entry multiples, integration capacity, and technician labor — not end-market demand.

## SECTION 3 — DEMAND: THE DATA CENTER SUPERCYCLE AND OTHER STRUCTURAL DRIVERS

### 3.1 The Data Center Supercycle

The dominant force reshaping commercial mechanical demand is hyperscale data-center construction, and the primary data is unambiguous. Private U.S. data-center construction reached a **$50.7 billion seasonally adjusted annual rate in April 2026, up 28.1% year over year** (U.S. Census Bureau, reported by Bloomberg, June 1, 2026) — surpassing general office construction (~$43.8 billion SAAR) for the first time and roughly doubling over two years. Comfort Systems' technology-sector customers accounted for **45% of full-year 2025 revenue, up from 33%** the year prior — and reached 56% of revenue by Q1 2026 — while its backlog hit $11.94 billion at year-end 2025 (a 99% annual increase) and $12.45 billion by March 31, 2026. Legence went public in September 2025 with roughly 40% of its backlog tied to data-center contracts. EMCOR's remaining performance obligations set records through the same period.

Capex forecasts frame the runway, and here analysts differ — we cite both: CreditSights projected **~$602 billion of 2026 capex for the top five U.S. hyperscalers** as of January 2026, then **raised its estimate to ~$750 billion** after the Q4 2025 earnings cycle; other trackers cite ~$725 billion for the big four. Epoch AI estimates a single one-gigawatt AI data center requires approximately **$38 billion in upfront capital expenditure**. McKinsey (April 2025) projects total data-center capacity rising from 82.3 GW in 2025 to 219.0 GW by 2030 — a 22% annual growth rate — with mechanical and electrical systems capex "likely to exceed $250 billion by 2030." Power-demand forecasts diverge by forecaster (EPRI, McKinsey, SemiAnalysis, DOE/LBNL): commonly cited trajectories run from ~31 GW of U.S. data-center demand in 2025 toward ~66 GW by 2027, with long-range 2035 scenarios as high as ~194 GW (~20% of U.S. electricity). Different forecasters report materially different numbers; the direction — steep, sustained growth constrained primarily by power availability — is not in dispute.

Why this accrues to mechanical contractors: the building shell is cheap; the systems are not. MEP infrastructure is estimated to consume **60–70% of a data center's development budget** — electrical systems 25–35% of facility cost, mechanical and cooling another 20–25% — and moving from N+1 to 2N fault-tolerant redundancy can raise electrical and mechanical costs 40–60% (engineering/practitioner estimates). Grid constraints are redistributing the geography: per JLL's North America Data Center Report (Year-End 2025), Texas alone has 6.5 GW under construction and is positioned to overtake Virginia as the world's largest data-center market by 2030, while **64% of the 35 GW North American pipeline now sits in frontier markets** (Texas, Tennessee, Wisconsin, Ohio), with vacancy at a record-low 1% and 92% of the pipeline pre-committed.

### 3.2 The Liquid Cooling Transition — Where Trade Skill Becomes Multiple

Rack power density has broken the air-cooling paradigm. Legacy commercial data centers ran 5–15 kW per rack, comfortably served by CRAC/CRAH units and hot/cold-aisle containment. Current-generation AI clusters run far hotter: NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 architecture draws approximately **120 kW per rack nominal (130–132 kW at full load** per HPE and Vertiv/NVIDIA reference designs; earlier reporting citing ~165 kW is unsupported for this generation). Above roughly 20 kW per rack, air collapses as an economical heat-transfer medium, forcing a mandatory industry transition to liquid cooling.

| Cooling architecture | Optimal rack density | Mechanism | Contractor requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional air (CRAC/CRAH) | <20 kW | Hot/cold aisle containment, chilled water, fan arrays | Legacy commercial HVAC |
| Rear-door heat exchangers (RDHx) | 20–80 kW+ (two-phase to ~120 kW) | Liquid-cooled coil replaces cabinet rear door; no liquid to IT hardware | Facility water loops; the retrofit bridge |
| Direct-to-chip, single-phase | 50–150 kW | Coolant through cold plates on CPU/GPU | CDUs, manifolds, dripless quick-disconnects, leak detection |
| Direct-to-chip, two-phase | 100 kW–1 MW+ | Dielectric fluid boils at the chip (latent heat) | Engineered closed-loop refrigerant systems |
| Immersion | 100 kW+ | Servers submerged in dielectric fluid | Purpose-built tanks; altered maintenance workflows |

Analysts size the liquid-cooling market differently — we cite the range: Grand View Research projects the global data-center liquid-cooling market from **$6.6 billion (2025) to $29.5 billion by 2033 (20.1% CAGR)**; MarketsandMarkets projects ~$27.65 billion by 2033; MarkNtel Advisors projects $5.51 billion (2025) to $19.03 billion by 2030 (~23% CAGR); other estimates place the 2025/26 base as low as ~$4.1 billion. Whatever the precise figure, two facts matter for acquirers: the service and maintenance segment of liquid cooling is projected to be the fastest-growing component (continuous optimization, water-quality management, and fault-tolerance maintenance of mission-critical thermal loops is recurring revenue by construction), and the pipefitting precision, fluid-dynamics competence, and clean-room discipline it requires are exceptionally scarce in the contractor base. Contractors with proven liquid-loop capability transact above standard commercial valuation curves.

### 3.3 Other Structural Drivers

**Refrigerant transition (AIM Act).** The AIM Act mandates an 85% HFC phasedown over 15 years. EPA's Technology Transitions Rule set a 700-GWP limit for new systems: R-410A is restricted in new equipment manufactured after December 31, 2024, with R-454B and R-32 as successors, and installation and leak-repair/HFC-management requirements phasing in through January 1, 2026. This drives a multi-year commercial replacement and retrofit cycle and requires EPA 608 plus A2L-certified technicians. One caution: EPA announced reconsideration of the Technology Transitions Rule during 2025, so thresholds and dates may shift; treat retrofit-wave sizing as directional.

**Water and municipal infrastructure.** The EPA-identified $625 billion, 20-year drinking-water need (plus ~$630 billion wastewater/stormwater) underwrites the commercial/municipal plumbing adjacency independent of the construction cycle.

**Electrification, decarbonization, and controls.** Heat-pump conversion, building performance standards, and energy performance contracting are shifting commercial HVAC from break-fix toward outcome-based service models. IoT sensors, building automation systems, and AI-driven predictive maintenance are capturing a growing share of facility capex; smart HVAC controls and performance-contracting models show the sector's highest growth rates, with published CAGRs approaching 15%. The median U.S. commercial building was 44 years old in 2025 (Mordor), sustaining retrofit demand independent of new construction.

**Materials transition (plumbing).** Advanced polymers (PPR, PE, uPVC) are displacing metal piping in commercial applications, cutting installation labor time by an estimated 35% versus conventional metal pipe; commercial buildings account for roughly 26% of global pipe-and-fittings demand (market-research estimates).

**Industrial reshoring.** Manufacturing construction spending remains historically elevated (~$220 billion in 2025 per Census-based reporting, pending primary verification), though semiconductor and EV megaprojects are transitioning from peak buildout toward fit-out and service tails.

### 3.4 Headwinds

**Labor is the binding constraint.** BLS projects HVAC-R mechanic employment up 8% from 2024 to 2034 with ~40,100 annual openings, and plumber/pipefitter/steamfitter employment up 4% with ~44,000 annual openings; the plumber median wage is $62,970 per year (May 2024 OOH). Industry sources cite a shortage on the order of 110,000 HVAC technicians with ~25,000 leaving the field annually. Comfort Systems management summarized the sector's position: the challenge ahead is execution, not order intake. (BLS occupational data blends residential and commercial and cannot be cleanly separated.)

**Cyclicality and concentration.** Private nonresidential construction spending plateaued through early 2026 outside of data centers. The sector's re-rating is heavily concentrated in one end market whose capex has not been tested by a downcycle at this scale. Wage inflation, tariffs on steel, aluminum, copper, and imported equipment, and grid interconnection delays round out the risk set.

## SECTION 9 — DUE DILIGENCE: THE COMMERCIAL-SPECIFIC RISK STACK

### 9.1 The WIP Schedule and Percentage-of-Completion Accounting

The foundation of commercial mechanical diligence is the work-in-progress schedule. Commercial contractors recognize revenue and gross profit progressively as costs are incurred under percent-complete accounting; the integrity of reported EBITDA therefore rests entirely on the accuracy of internal cost-to-complete estimates. A project manager who underestimates remaining labor or materials inflates the percentage of completion, prematurely recognizes revenue and gross profit, and overstates trailing EBITDA. Quality-of-earnings work must test the historical accuracy of the WIP schedule: do project managers update budgets dynamically, or do they recognize overruns only at closeout?

### 9.2 Under-Billings, Over-Billings, and the Working-Capital Peg

Percent-complete accounting creates two balance-sheet accounts that dominate purchase-price mechanics. Under-billings (costs and estimated earnings in excess of billings) represent revenue recognized but not yet invoiced; aged or chronic under-billings signal disputed change orders, unapproved scope, or rejected claims, and buyers reverse the associated revenue out of normalized EBITDA. Over-billings (billings in excess of costs) reflect front-loaded invoicing — excellent cash management for the seller, but the buyer inherits a legal obligation to perform work whose cash the seller already collected. The defense is the net-working-capital peg: a normalized level of receivables, inventory, and net billings required to run the business post-close, with any shortfall at closing reducing the purchase price dollar for dollar. In commercial mechanical deals, turns of value are won and lost here, not in the headline multiple.

### 9.3 Margin Fade and Backlog Underwriting

Margin fade — the deterioration of estimated gross margin from bid through completion, driven by labor productivity, supply-chain delays, and estimating error — is the standard failure mode. Sophisticated buyers do not pay for top-line backlog; they underwrite its execution quality. A $50 million contracted backlog is functionally worthless if the firm bids at 15% margins and completes at 8%, or lacks the labor to execute it. A historical fade pattern in the WIP schedule discounts the entire forward book.

### 9.4 Multiemployer Pension Withdrawal Liability

For union signatories (United Association, SMART), the single most dangerous diligence item is multiemployer pension withdrawal liability under ERISA as amended by the MPPAA of 1980. Contributing employers are strictly liable for their proportionate share of a plan's unfunded vested benefits — a statutory exit tax triggered by ceasing contributions through shutdown, decertification, or certain transactions. The calculation is acutely sensitive to the actuary's discount-rate assumption, and the law here was just settled at the highest level: on May 21, 2026, the **U.S. Supreme Court decided *M&K Employee Solutions, LLC v. Trustees of the IAM National Pension Fund*** (No. 23-1209), affirming the D.C. Circuit and holding that ERISA does not require the actuarial assumptions used to calculate withdrawal liability to be locked on or before the statutory measurement date — the "as of" language fixes the factual inputs, while assumptions remain predictive tools subject to a reasonableness and best-estimate standard. The decision resolved a circuit split with the Second Circuit's *Metz* line and preserves funds' latitude on discount rates; assessments can rival or exceed a contractor's enterprise value.

Exposure also reaches beyond the signatory entity: under ERISA's controlled-group rules, all trades or businesses under common control are jointly and severally liable (applying the *Groetzinger* trade-or-business test), and case law — including the Sixth Circuit's *Art Iron* decision — has reached spousal side businesses and real-estate holding companies (citation to be verified by counsel before external use).

Two statutory safe harbors govern deal structuring. The building-and-construction-industry exemption (ERISA §4203(b)) avoids withdrawal liability on cessation if substantially all — interpreted as at least 85% — of contributed-for employees perform on-site building and construction work (fabrication-shop, modular, and delivery labor often fail the test, a live litigation risk for prefab-heavy contractors), and the employer does not resume the same work in the same jurisdiction non-union for five years. For asset sales, §4204 preserves the seller's exemption if the buyer continues substantially the same contribution base units, posts a bond or escrow equal to one year's average contributions for five plan years, and the agreement holds the seller secondarily liable for five years — with the buyer inheriting the seller's five-year contribution history, effectively transferring the risk. Stock purchases generally avoid triggering withdrawal but import every legacy liability. Practical rule: obtain withdrawal-liability estimates from every fund annually and treat the exposure as debt-like in the purchase price. (Specific case citations — *Dycom*, *Walker Specialty*, *SuperValu* — should be verified by ERISA counsel before external publication.)

### 9.5 Bonding, Concentration, and the Remaining Checklist

Surety programs do not transfer automatically; personal indemnities must be released, and bonding capacity is the growth governor for construction-weighted firms. Customer or GC concentration above 20–25% is common and heavily repriced. Prevailing-wage/Davis-Bacon capability (certified payroll systems) widens the addressable bid universe; its absence narrows it. The remaining checklist: EPA 608 and A2L technician compliance, EMR below 0.85, reviewed or audited financials, owner-independent management, and clean WIP-to-GL reconciliation.

## SECTION 12 — INVESTMENT THESIS AND PLATFORM PLAYBOOK

### 12.1 The Thesis, Restated

Commercial mechanical is where residential HVAC stood roughly a decade ago — deeply fragmented, early in its consolidation cycle, with an under-exploited recurring-revenue arbitrage — except it is riding the largest structural demand shock in the history of the trades. The buyers who can underwrite WIP schedules, bonding transfer, and pension liability will buy at clearing multiples what sells, once converted to service and credentialed for mission-critical work, at a premium of several turns. The complexity is not a bug; it is the moat that keeps tourist capital out.

### 12.2 The Staged Playbook

**Stage 1 — Platform selection (months 0–6).** Target a founder-owned commercial mechanical services contractor with more than 30% recurring service/PMA revenue, $3–8M EBITDA (or $5–25M for a larger anchor), in a data-center/reshoring corridor or underconsolidated metro — Texas ex-majors, the Mountain West, secondary Southeast. Require a documented PMA renewal rate above 85%, service gross margin above 30%, bonding capacity appropriate to the construction book, EMR below 0.85, and reviewed financials. Entry target 6–8x. Threshold: if no 40%-service-mix platform is available inside 8x, anchor on a project-heavy contractor at 5–6x and underwrite the service conversion explicitly.

**Stage 2 — Buy and build (months 6–48).** Execute three to eight add-ons per year at 4–7x, prioritizing service-heavy tuck-ins, geographic density within 60–90 miles, and controls/BAS capability. Impose sell-side-grade QoE on every target's WIP schedule; negotiate working-capital pegs and backlog-conversion earnouts to de-risk plan-spec revenue; run §4204 mechanics on every union asset. Benchmark cadence against FirstCall (23 branches in ~3 years) and Pueblo/Modigent (16 bolt-ons in ~4 years). Threshold: if add-on/platform arbitrage compresses below two turns, slow the cadence and push organic service conversion.

**Stage 3 — Mix shift and re-rate (ongoing).** Drive recurring service past 40% of gross profit — the level that moves the asset across the valuation frameworks' dividing line. Centralize dispatch, procurement, and prefabrication. Solve labor deliberately: apprenticeship pipelines, union relationships where advantageous, and technician retention treated as an acquired asset. Track revenue per technician ($250K+ blended), billable efficiency (45–50%+), PMA renewal rate, and backlog margin quality.

**Stage 4 — Exit (years 4–6).** Position for a public strategic (Comfort Systems, EMCOR, Legence) or a larger sponsor. A platform at $15–30M+ EBITDA with more than 40–50% service mix, mission-critical credentials, and clean WIP should target 10–13x, with premium-process outcomes above that; high but concentrated data-center exposure will draw a concentration haircut, so document qualification transferability.

**Buyer-type routing.** Family offices: the commercial plumbing whitespace and municipal/water adjacency (patient capital). Independent sponsors and search funds: single-metro founder platforms in the Mountain West or secondary Southeast. LMM PE: multi-metro service-led platforms with controls attach and data-center exposure. Existing platforms: controls/BAS, refrigeration, and data-center-credentialed tuck-ins.

**Diligence non-negotiables:** WIP-to-GL reconciliation with gross-margin fade analysis; surety letter and bonding transfer plan; withdrawal-liability estimates from every multiemployer fund, treated as debt-like; customer/GC concentration; A2L/EPA 608 compliance; certified-payroll capability for prevailing-wage work.
