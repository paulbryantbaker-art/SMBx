# THE QUIET REPRICING
## How Commercial Mechanical Became an Infrastructure Asset Class — and What That Means for Buyers Through 2030

**A Buy-Side Market Assessment | smbX.ai | July 2026**

**Scope: U.S. commercial and nonresidential mechanical, HVAC, and plumbing contracting and services**

*Figures throughout are labeled **PRIMARY**, **INSTITUTIONAL**, or **DERIVED**. Method and sources appear at the end of the report.*

---

## THE SHORT VERSION

A fragmented, cyclical construction trade is being repriced as infrastructure, and the repricing is roughly halfway done.

Three things happened at once. Artificial intelligence created a demand shock the trade cannot staff — private data-center construction hit a **$50.7 billion annualized rate in April 2026, up 28.1% year over year**, passing general office construction for the first time in the history of the series. Institutional capital worked out that a subscription business is hiding inside a construction business: the recurring maintenance half of a mechanical contractor behaves like software revenue and prices accordingly. And the residential trades got picked over, pushing capital toward the commercial side, where the consolidation cycle is years behind.

The market is real but smaller than the numbers in circulation. Building up from Census establishment data, U.S. commercial mechanical, HVAC, and plumbing contracting is **roughly $180 billion in 2026**, of which about **$40 billion is recurring service and maintenance** — the segment that platforms are actually built on. Roughly **104,000 firms** compete in the space, the overwhelming majority under $10 million in revenue. The largest dedicated platform in the country holds low-single-digit share.

The opportunity is not "buy an HVAC company." It is a specific, repeatable arbitrage. The same business trades at very different prices depending on what share of its revenue is contracted rather than bid — roughly **6x for a project-led lower-middle-market contractor, 8 to 11x once it carries a real service book and data-center credentials** — and the conversion between those two states is an operational problem, not a market-timing problem. Two public companies, Limbach and APi Group, have run the play in front of everyone.

What protects the trade is that it is genuinely hard to underwrite. Percentage-of-completion accounting, working-capital pegs, surety transfer, and multiemployer pension withdrawal liability — a subject the Supreme Court addressed in May 2026 — keep generalist capital out. That difficulty is the moat. It is also, for a buyer with real corporate-development capability, the entire edge.

Through 2030, demand is close to locked: **JLL projects nearly 100 gigawatts of new data-center capacity between 2026 and 2030, doubling global capacity, with 77% of the construction pipeline already pre-committed to tenants.** Supply of technicians is not locked, and will not be. That asymmetry — contracted demand against a constrained labor base — is what should shape every underwriting model in this sector for the rest of the decade.

# PART I — HOW A TRADE BECAME AN ASSET CLASS

For most of its history, commercial mechanical contracting was a business nobody wanted to own from the outside. It was cyclical, tied to construction starts. It was capital-hungry in the worst way, funding labor and materials months before collecting. Its accounting was opaque to anyone who had not lived in it. And its earnings could evaporate in a single badly estimated project.

Then three things changed, roughly simultaneously.

**The demand shock arrived from an unexpected direction.** Data centers were a rounding error in commercial construction a decade ago. In April 2026, private data-center construction ran at a **$50.7 billion seasonally adjusted annual rate, up 28.1% year over year**, surpassing general office construction (approximately $43.8 billion) for the first time (PRIMARY — U.S. Census Bureau, reported by Bloomberg, June 1, 2026). This mattered disproportionately to mechanical contractors because of where the money goes in a data center: the shell is inexpensive, and the systems are not. Mechanical and electrical infrastructure absorbs the majority of a hyperscale facility's development budget, and cooling is the binding physical constraint on how much compute a building can hold.

The effect on the public companies is unmistakable. Comfort Systems USA's technology-sector customers accounted for **45% of full-year 2025 revenue, up from 33% the year before**, and its backlog reached **$11.94 billion at year-end 2025, a 99% increase** over the prior year (PRIMARY — company filings). EMCOR's remaining performance obligations reached **$13.25 billion at December 31, 2025, up 31.2%** year over year. Legence went public in September 2025 carrying a backlog heavily weighted to data-center work.

**Institutional capital found the recurring revenue.** Inside every commercial mechanical contractor sit two different businesses wearing the same name. One bids projects competitively, executes them over months, and starts again from zero. The other holds multi-year maintenance agreements on equipment that cannot be allowed to fail — hospital chiller plants, data-hall cooling loops, university campuses — and generates repair and replacement work through those agreements without ever seeing a competitive bid.

The second business is a subscription business. Once buyers understood that, they began pricing the two halves differently, and a generation of operators began deliberately converting from one to the other. Limbach Holdings took owner-direct relationships to **75.1% of revenue** by FY2025 and grew adjusted EBITDA 28.4% to $81.8 million on revenue of $646.8 million. APi Group moved inspection, service, and monitoring from roughly 40% of revenue in 2021 to **54% in 2025**, expanding adjusted EBITDA margin from 10.3% to 13.2% along the way (PRIMARY — company filings). Neither transformation required a market call. Both required operating discipline.

**And the residential trades filled up.** Private equity spent the better part of a decade consolidating residential HVAC and plumbing. That work is now well advanced, entry multiples reflect it, and the obvious targets have been bought. Commercial is the same trade with harder accounting, larger customers, and a consolidation cycle several years behind — which is precisely why capital is moving. Capstone Partners counted **149 HVAC transactions in 2025, up 12.9% year over year**, in what it describes as a widely fragmented market. S&P Global Market Intelligence reported private equity add-on activity in HVAC services **up 88% year over year** through mid-2025, with just over half of all transactions in the sector backed by financial sponsors or their portfolio companies (INSTITUTIONAL).

The convergence of those three forces is the story of this market. What follows is an attempt to size it honestly, map it, price it, and say plainly where the openings are.

# PART II — HOW BIG IS IT, REALLY

There is no official statistic for "the U.S. commercial mechanical market," which is why the figures in circulation vary by more than an order of magnitude. This section builds the number from the ground up and shows the arithmetic.

## The number most often cited, and why this report does not use it

Comfort Systems USA's 10-K contains a sentence that has become the industry's default market size. Its exact form, year by year (PRIMARY — SEC filings):

| Filing | Stated market size |
|---|---|
| FY2021 10-K | ~$300 billion |
| FY2023 10-K | ~$350 billion |
| FY2024 10-K | ~$550 billion |
| FY2025 10-K | ~$700 billion |

The sentence structure is identical each year: *"We believe that commercial, industrial, and institutional mechanical **and electrical** contracting generate annual revenue in the United States of approximately $X billion."*

Two problems make this unusable as an analytical base. First, it bundles electrical contracting with mechanical, so it does not describe the market this report covers. Second, and more seriously, it more than doubled in four years. No market does that. The figure grew as the company's own scope broadened — Comfort Systems expanded materially into electrical after 2019 — and as data-center and reshoring demand redefined what management counted. It is a self-generated estimate with no cited third-party source, and its own volatility is the argument against leaning on it.

It remains a useful directional signal of how a well-informed operator sees its opportunity expanding. It is not a market size.

## Building the number from Census data

**Step 1 — The revenue base (PRIMARY).** The Economic Census measures actual revenue at actual establishments. NAICS 238220, Plumbing, Heating and Air-Conditioning Contractors, reported **$207.9 billion in value of business done, 1,028,117 employees, and $60.4 billion in payroll** in the most recent complete industry series. This covers all customer types, residential and commercial together.

**Step 2 — Grow it forward (DERIVED).** Two forces move the base from the Census year to 2026: establishment and employment growth (County Business Patterns shows 106,854 establishments and 1,124,239 employees by 2020) and price inflation, which has been severe — the BLS Producer Price Index for new nonresidential building construction by plumbing, heating and air-conditioning contractors reached **150.98 in February 2026** on a 2014 = 100 base.

Applying a composite factor of approximately 1.65x:

> $207.9B × 1.65 ≈ **$343B**, with continued real growth bringing the 2026 midpoint to approximately **$355 billion** for all of NAICS 238220.

**Step 3 — Isolate the commercial half (DERIVED).** The Census publishes the residential-versus-nonresidential split for this industry in its value-of-construction-work-by-type-of-construction series. That table is the definitive answer and should be extracted directly before the next revision of this report. In its absence, this analysis uses a 50% nonresidential share and shows the sensitivity:

| Nonresidential share | 2026 commercial TAM |
|---|---|
| 45% | ~$160 billion |
| **50% — base case** | **~$180 billion** |
| 55% | ~$195 billion |

> **Total addressable market: approximately $180 billion (range $160–210 billion).**

The band around that midpoint is driven almost entirely by the nonresidential share assumption. Every other input is measured.

## What sits inside the number

The Census divides contractor work into new construction, additions and alterations, and maintenance and repair. Blending that structure with the service mixes public companies actually disclose — Comfort Systems at 63.2% new construction and 36.8% existing-building work; Legence deriving 21% of installation-and-maintenance revenue from maintenance and service — produces the following decomposition (DERIVED):

| Segment | Share | 2026 commercial revenue | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| New construction and installation | ~50% | ~$90 billion | Bid, cyclical, working-capital intensive |
| Renovation, retrofit, replacement | ~28% | ~$50 billion | Mixed; often negotiated |
| **Recurring maintenance and service** | **~22%** | **~$40 billion** | **Contracted, sticky, highest multiple** |

That **$40 billion recurring service pool is the real prize.** It is where the pricing premium lives, where customer relationships persist through downturns, and where nearly every platform in this report has concentrated its acquisitions.

## The acquirable universe

Census Statistics of U.S. Businesses counts approximately **104,000 firms** in NAICS 238220 (PRIMARY). The distribution is heavily weighted toward micro-businesses; roughly 28,000 establishments fall in the 5-to-49 employee band.

Converting employment bands to revenue at approximately $260,000–$290,000 per employee for 2026, the $2 million to $250 million revenue window — the range where institutional buyers actually transact — captures roughly **18,000 to 22,000 firms**. Applying a commercial-focus filter of approximately 35% (DERIVED):

> **Serviceable addressable market: approximately 6,000 to 7,000 acquirable commercial-focused firms, representing roughly $100 billion of revenue.**

## What a single platform can realistically take

Benchmarked against what platforms have actually achieved rather than what sponsors project, a well-capitalized buy-and-build over a four-to-six-year hold can reasonably reach **$300 million to $1 billion of revenue** — between 0.3% and 0.6% of the market. Legence compounded revenue at roughly 27% annually from 2022 to 2025, reaching $2.55 billion. Service Logic assembled 140-plus locations and more than 5,000 technicians across multiple sponsor holds.

The constraints on that trajectory are capital, deal flow in fragmented secondary metros, and technician recruiting. Demand is not among them.

## The three numbers to carry forward

| | Figure | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **TAM** | ~$180B commercial mechanical contracting | Census-derived, 2026 |
| **Recurring service core** | ~$40B | Census work-type structure + filed service mixes |
| **SAM** | ~$100B across ~6,000–7,000 firms | Census SUSB size bands |

# PART III — THE DEMAND SHOCK

## Data centers, and why cooling is the bottleneck

The constraint on artificial intelligence is not chips. It is power and heat rejection, and both land on the mechanical trade.

The scale is documented in primary data. The Department of Energy's Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that U.S. data centers consumed **176 terawatt-hours in 2023 — about 4.4% of total U.S. electricity — up from 58 terawatt-hours in 2014, and projected to reach 325 to 580 terawatt-hours by 2028, or 6.7% to 12% of national consumption** (PRIMARY — *2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report*, December 2024). This is the authoritative federal estimate and the one this report uses; the wider ranges quoted elsewhere come from forecasters with less transparent methods.

Forward capacity is equally well documented. JLL's 2026 Global Data Center Outlook projects **nearly 100 gigawatts of new capacity between 2026 and 2030, roughly doubling global capacity from about 103 gigawatts to 200 gigawatts — a 14% compound annual growth rate — requiring as much as $3 trillion of investment** (INSTITUTIONAL). The two statistics in that report that matter most to a buyer are not the headline numbers: **global occupancy stands at 97%, and 77% of the construction pipeline is already pre-committed to tenants.** There is effectively no slack in this market, and the demand is contracted rather than speculative.

Every gigawatt of that capacity requires chilled water, piping, pumps, controls, and technicians who can commission and maintain it. And as rack densities climb past the point where moving air is economical, the work shifts toward liquid cooling — coolant distribution units, manifolds, dripless quick-disconnects, leak detection, water chemistry management. That work demands pipefitting precision and clean-room discipline that a conventional commercial HVAC crew does not possess.

The honest position on liquid cooling market size: **no credible primary source exists.** Vendor estimates put the 2033 figure anywhere from $19 billion to $29.5 billion, which is a wide enough spread to make the number meaningless. What can be said with confidence is qualitative and, for an acquirer, more useful: liquid-cooling capability is scarce, the maintenance component is recurring by construction, and contractors who hold the capability price above the market.

## What a sophisticated buyer just paid to enter this space

Two 2025 transactions reveal how institutional capital is valuing mechanical and critical-systems services with data-center exposure (PRIMARY — company disclosures):

**CBRE agreed to acquire Pearce Services from New Mountain Capital for approximately $1.2 billion in cash plus an earn-out of up to $115 million** (announced November 4, 2025). Pearce projected more than $660 million of 2026 revenue and more than $90 million of EBITDA, with critical power and cooling systems representing 34% of expected 2025 revenue. CBRE told investors the deal expands its addressable market **by more than $30 billion.**

**Dycom acquired Power Solutions for approximately $1.95 billion** — roughly $1.63 billion cash plus about 1.0 million shares — at **9.7 times trailing four-quarter adjusted EBITDA, reduced to approximately 8.5 times net of expected tax benefits** (announced December 23, 2025; approximately $1.0 billion expected 2025 revenue).

These are the clearest public marks available for what critical-infrastructure services businesses trade for at scale.

## The drivers that do not depend on data centers

A thesis resting entirely on hyperscale construction is a fragile thesis. Three other demand sources operate on independent clocks.

**Refrigerant transition.** The AIM Act mandates an 85% phasedown of hydrofluorocarbons, and EPA's Technology Transitions Rule set a 700-GWP ceiling for new equipment, restricting R-410A in new systems manufactured after December 31, 2024 in favor of R-454B and R-32. Commercial building owners face a multi-year replacement and retrofit cycle, and the technicians who perform it need A2L certification on top of EPA 608. *One flag: EPA opened a reconsideration of the Technology Transitions Rule in 2025, and its current status should be confirmed against the Federal Register before this driver is sized in any investment committee memo.*

**Water infrastructure.** EPA's 7th Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey identifies **$625 billion of drinking-water infrastructure need over 20 years**, a 32% increase over the prior assessment, with roughly $630 billion more in wastewater and stormwater need per the 2022 Clean Watersheds Needs Survey (PRIMARY). This is publicly funded, counter-cyclical demand that underwrites the commercial and municipal plumbing adjacency regardless of what commercial real estate does.

**Aging building stock and performance mandates.** The existing commercial building inventory is old, energy codes are tightening jurisdiction by jurisdiction, and building performance standards are spreading through major metros. This converts mechanical contractors from equipment installers into performance partners — the outcome-based service model that Legence and Crete United both built brands around.

## The constraint that will not resolve

Every demand driver above runs into the same wall. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects HVAC and refrigeration mechanic employment **up 8% from 2024 to 2034, with roughly 40,100 openings annually**, and plumbers, pipefitters and steamfitters **up 4%, with roughly 44,000 openings annually** (PRIMARY — BLS Employment Projections; note that occupational data blends residential and commercial and cannot be cleanly separated).

Those openings are driven substantially by retirement, not growth. The trade is not producing replacements fast enough, and no amount of capital changes that in a five-year window.

For an acquirer, this cuts two ways. It caps how fast any platform can grow organically, and it makes wage inflation a live margin risk. It also means a trained, retained technician base is a genuinely scarce asset — which is a large part of what a buyer is actually paying for, and a reason that retention economics deserve as much diligence attention as the WIP schedule.

Brokerage materials circulate tiered tables reaching 13 to 20 times EBITDA for larger commercial mechanical firms. ## A single framework, with the conditions attached

The apparent contradiction between "6x" and "16x" dissolves once you specify what is being measured. The same asset prices differently depending on five identifiable factors: size, process type, service mix, end-market exposure, and management depth.

| Situation | Range | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Proprietary add-on, no process, owner-dependent | 5–7x | No competitive tension; buyer absorbs integration risk |
| Lower-middle-market platform, $10–25M TEV, mixed project and service | 6.0–6.4x | GF Data closed-deal average |
| Mid-market, $50–100M TEV | ~8.3x | GF Data closed-deal average |
| **Specialty trade with data-center or critical-power exposure** | **8–11x** | **Dycom/Power Solutions at 9.7x is the cleanest public mark** |
| Large platform, $250–500M TEV | ~10.1x | GF Data closed-deal average |
| Service-heavy platform, scarce capability, banked competitive auction | 12–16x+ | Platform scarcity pricing — the exception, not the benchmark |

**Underwrite entry against the closed-deal rows. Treat the bottom row as an exit scenario a transformed asset can argue for, not an assumption.**

## The service-mix premium, sized honestly

The claim that converting to recurring service revenue re-rates a business is correct. The claim that it is worth five turns is not well supported by published transaction evidence.

What the evidence supports: service-heavy platforms trade at the top of the specialty band rather than the middle, and GF Data consistently records premiums for recurring-revenue characteristics. In practice this is worth roughly **two to three turns** — the difference between a 6x project-led lower-middle-market contractor and an 8-to-9x service-led one — widening toward the high end only when the maintenance book is genuinely contracted, renewing, and attached to mission-critical equipment.

Two to three turns on $5 million of EBITDA is $10 to $15 million of enterprise value created through operating discipline rather than market timing. That is a large enough prize that it does not need to be oversold.

# PART VI — WHY IT IS HARD, AND WHY THAT IS THE POINT

Commercial mechanical companies fail in diligence for reasons that do not arise in most lower-middle-market deals. A buyer who cannot navigate them should not be in this sector. A buyer who can enjoys structurally less competition.

**The WIP schedule is the whole game.** Commercial contractors recognize revenue as costs are incurred against estimated total cost. Reported EBITDA is therefore only as accurate as the project managers' cost-to-complete estimates. A manager who understates remaining cost inflates percentage of completion and pulls profit forward. Quality-of-earnings work must test whether estimates were updated dynamically through each job or whether overruns surfaced only at closeout. A firm with the second pattern is reporting earnings it has not made.

**Billings positions move the purchase price.** Under-billings — revenue recognized but not invoiced — often represent disputed change orders or unapproved scope, and buyers reverse them out of normalized earnings. Over-billings mean the seller has already collected cash for work the buyer must still perform. Both flow into the net working capital peg, and a shortfall at closing reduces the price dollar for dollar. More value is won and lost in the peg negotiation than in the headline multiple.

**Backlog is not an asset until it is executed.** Margin fade — the drift from bid margin to as-built margin — is the standard failure mode. A firm that bids at 15% and completes at 8% has a backlog worth roughly half what it appears to be worth. Historical fade patterns discount the entire forward book, and a firm without the labor to execute its backlog does not really have one.

**Pension exposure can exceed enterprise value.** For union signatories, multiemployer pension withdrawal liability under ERISA is the single most dangerous item on the list. Contributing employers are strictly liable for their share of a plan's unfunded vested benefits, and the calculation is acutely sensitive to the actuary's discount rate. **The Supreme Court addressed this directly in May 2026, holding that ERISA does not require actuarial assumptions to be fixed as of the statutory measurement date — the "as of" language fixes the factual inputs, while assumptions remain predictive tools subject to a reasonableness standard.** The practical effect is that funds retain meaningful latitude in setting discount rates, and assessments can rival or exceed a contractor's enterprise value.

Exposure extends beyond the signatory entity under ERISA's controlled-group rules, and structuring matters enormously: stock purchases generally avoid triggering withdrawal but import every legacy liability, while asset sales require Section 4204 mechanics — the buyer continuing substantially the same contribution base, posting bond or escrow, and the seller remaining secondarily liable for five years. Obtain withdrawal-liability estimates from every fund annually and treat the exposure as debt-like. *All ERISA structuring should be confirmed with specialist counsel; this report describes the framework, not the advice.*

**Everything else.** Surety programs do not transfer automatically and personal indemnities must be released. Customer or general-contractor concentration above 20–25% is repriced heavily. Prevailing-wage capability requires certified payroll systems. And the diligence list closes with technician retention, EMR below 0.85, reviewed financials, and management that functions without the founder.

None of this is exotic to someone who has run these deals. All of it is opaque to someone who has not. That gap is worth more to a disciplined buyer than any market forecast in this report.

# PART VII — THE 2030 OPPORTUNITY

Four years is roughly one hold period. This section states what can reasonably be assumed about 2030 and where the openings are for the two kinds of capital most active in this space.

## What is close to locked

**Demand is contracted, not forecast.** This is the single most important fact for underwriting. JLL reports **97% global data-center occupancy and 77% of the construction pipeline already pre-committed to tenants**, against nearly 100 gigawatts of new capacity planned between 2026 and 2030. The DOE/Berkeley Lab projection of **325 to 580 terawatt-hours of U.S. data-center consumption by 2028** is a federal estimate, not a vendor projection. Comfort Systems' backlog nearly doubled in one year; EMCOR's remaining performance obligations rose 31.2%. Demand of this kind is visible years in advance because it is contracted years in advance.

**Regulatory demand is legislated.** The AIM Act phasedown and the EPA-identified $625 billion drinking-water need do not depend on interest rates or commercial real estate sentiment.

**Labor scarcity persists.** BLS projections through 2034 show modest employment growth against large replacement demand. No policy or capital response changes the technician supply curve inside four years.

## What is genuinely uncertain

**Whether data-center capex holds.** The sector's re-rating traces substantially to one end market whose capital cycle has never been tested by a downturn at this scale. Pre-commitment mitigates but does not eliminate this. Any underwriting model should carry a case in which hyperscale construction decelerates sharply after 2028.

**Where multiples land.** Current levels sit somewhat below the 2020–2021 peak. A platform bought at 8x in 2026 and exited at 8x in 2030 has to create value operationally rather than through multiple expansion — which is the correct way to underwrite anyway.

**Whether wage inflation outruns pricing.** If technician compensation rises faster than contractors can reprice service agreements, margins compress across the sector. This is the most underappreciated risk in the space and deserves explicit sensitivity analysis.

## Where the openings are

**1. Commercial plumbing — the clearest whitespace in the sector.** No national pure-play consolidator exists. Every platform treats plumbing as a bolt-on to an HVAC-led strategy. The demand foundation is federally documented and counter-cyclical. Entry multiples reflect the absence of competition rather than the quality of the asset.
*Best suited to: family offices with patient capital and a tolerance for building rather than buying scale; independent sponsors.*

**2. Secondary metros — same trade, half the competition.** Mid-sized Midwest and interior South markets, and the Mountain West outside Phoenix, carry the same aging building stock and the same succession-driven seller supply as the data-center corridors, with a fraction of the buyer competition. A platform assembled here at 5–7x and sold as a regional leader with genuine density is the least glamorous and most reliable play in this report.
*Best suited to: independent sponsors, search funds, first-time platforms.*

**3. The service conversion — the repeatable arbitrage.** Buy project-led contractors at the low end of the clearing range, drive contracted maintenance past 40% of gross profit, and re-rate the asset by two to three turns. Publicly demonstrated by Limbach and APi. This is an operating problem with a known solution, not a market bet.
*Best suited to: lower-middle-market private equity with genuine operating capability; operator-acquirers.*

**4. Critical-power and cooling credentials — the scarcity premium.** Contractors qualified to install and maintain high-density and liquid-cooled environments are rare and price accordingly. CBRE paid roughly $1.2 billion to acquire this capability; Dycom paid 9.7x. Acquiring a smaller firm with these credentials before the market fully prices them is the highest-return, highest-risk play here.
*Best suited to: lower-middle-market PE and operator-acquirers with data-center relationships.*

**5. Building automation and controls attach.** High-margin, sticky, and constrained by specialized labor. Rarely a standalone platform; consistently accretive as a capability added to an existing service business.
*Best suited to: add-on for an existing platform.*

## How the two buyer types should read this differently

**Family offices** hold the structural advantage where patience is the scarce input: commercial plumbing, municipal and water-adjacent work, and founder businesses whose owners care about legacy and continuity more than clearing price. A family office does not have to exit in five years, which means it can buy the asset a sponsor cannot underwrite — the one with a great technician base, a lumpy earnings history, and no management depth. What family offices typically lack is the corporate-development function to source and diligence at volume.

**Lower-middle-market private equity** holds the advantage where speed and operating capability matter: service conversion, multi-metro density, controls attach, and the credentialed data-center work. The binding constraint is competition for the obvious assets in the obvious geographies — which is the argument for the secondary metros above.

**Both** face the same first-order problem, which has nothing to do with capital: knowing which forty companies in a lane actually matter, which owners are approaching a transition, and which businesses have a real service book rather than a customer list. That is market work, and it takes weeks before the first conversation.

# PART VIII — HOW TO PLAY IT

**Stage 1 — Platform selection, months 0–6.** Target a founder-owned commercial mechanical services contractor with more than 30% recurring service revenue and $3–8 million of EBITDA, in an underconsolidated metro. Require a documented maintenance-agreement renewal rate above 85%, service gross margin above 30%, bonding capacity appropriate to the construction book, EMR below 0.85, and reviewed financials. Entry target 6–8x.
*Threshold: if no platform with 40%-plus service mix is available inside 8x, anchor on a project-led contractor at 5–6x and underwrite the conversion explicitly rather than paying for a mix you can build.*

**Stage 2 — Buy and build, months 6–48.** Three to eight add-ons per year at 4–7x, prioritizing service-heavy tuck-ins, geographic density within 60–90 miles, and controls capability. Impose sell-side-grade quality-of-earnings work on every WIP schedule. Negotiate working-capital pegs and backlog-conversion earnouts. Run Section 4204 mechanics on every union asset.
*Threshold: if the spread between add-on and platform multiples compresses below two turns, slow the cadence and push organic conversion instead.*

**Stage 3 — Mix shift and re-rate, ongoing.** Drive contracted service past 40% of gross profit. Centralize dispatch, procurement, and prefabrication. Treat technician recruiting and retention as a capital allocation decision, not an HR function. Track revenue per technician, billable efficiency, maintenance-agreement renewal rate, and — above all — margin fade on completed work.

**Stage 4 — Exit, years 4–6.** A platform at $15–30 million of EBITDA with 40–50% service mix, mission-critical credentials, and a clean WIP history should clear 9–12x, with premium-process outcomes above that. Concentrated data-center exposure will draw a haircut unless qualifications and customer relationships demonstrably transfer.

**Diligence non-negotiables.** WIP-to-general-ledger reconciliation with margin fade analysis. Surety letter and bonding transfer plan. Withdrawal-liability estimates from every multiemployer fund, treated as debt-like. Customer and general-contractor concentration. Refrigerant certification compliance. Certified-payroll capability. Technician retention data by tenure.

# METHOD, LIMITATIONS, AND SOURCES

## How figures in this report are sourced

Every material figure carries one of three labels.

**PRIMARY** — U.S. government data (Census Bureau, Bureau of Labor Statistics, EPA, Department of Energy) or SEC filings.
**INSTITUTIONAL** — named forecasters and transaction databases with published methodology: JLL, McKinsey, DOE/Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, GF Data, Capstone Partners, CFMA.
**DERIVED** — calculated in this report from the sources above, with the arithmetic shown.

Where a figure could not be traced to one of those three, it was left out rather than caveated. Commercial market-research vendor estimates — the kind that price this industry anywhere from $18 billion to $500 billion depending on which page you read — are not used as a basis for anything here. Where they are mentioned at all, it is to explain why they were set aside.

The result is a smaller headline market size than the figures circulating in advisory materials, and a narrower valuation range than the ones in brokerage pitch decks. Both are deliberate. A number you can defend in a partner meeting is worth more than a number that flatters the opportunity.

## What we know, and what we do not

Three items carry genuine, bounded uncertainty. They are stated here rather than scattered through the text.

**The residential-versus-commercial split.** The Census publishes the definitive split for NAICS 238220 in its value-of-construction-work-by-type series. This report uses a 50% nonresidential assumption and shows the full sensitivity — a $160 billion to $210 billion range around the $180 billion midpoint. Extracting that table directly would convert the largest remaining assumption into measured data and is the highest-value refinement available to this analysis.

**The Service Logic transaction value.** No principal disclosed terms. MergerLinks reports approximately $4.1 billion. That figure is attributed, not confirmed, and should not be used as a comparable without that qualification.

**The status of EPA's Technology Transitions Rule reconsideration.** The AIM Act phasedown is statutory, but the specific GWP thresholds and compliance dates were reopened in 2025. Confirm current status against the Federal Register before sizing the retrofit opportunity in any investment memo.

Everything else in this report traces to U.S. government data, SEC filings, or named institutional sources with published methodology.
