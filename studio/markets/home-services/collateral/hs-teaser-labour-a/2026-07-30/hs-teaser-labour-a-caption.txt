The $33 billion annual cost of the plumber shortage is not in the study everyone cites it to.

I opened the study. Its number is $1.27 billion — and it is a savings figure, not a loss.

The research itself is careful. LIXIL and John Dunham & Associates, with economist Michael Flaherty, project roughly 550,000 unfilled plumbing positions by 2027, and identify $1.27 billion in annual potential savings from adding about 16,400 plumbers (Blocked Pipes, 2024; press release 11 March 2026).

That is an opportunity being quantified. Somewhere between the study and the slide it became a $33 billion cost, and it now circulates as the headline economic damage of the trades shortage.

The same thing happened to the other number in that thesis. JLL estimates 2.1 million unfilled skilled-trades positions by 2030 — that figure is theirs and it holds (JLL, 21 April 2026). The $1 trillion annual economic loss quoted alongside it is not. JLL attributes it to the U.S. Department of Education, and it should be cited that way.

None of this makes the shortage less real. BLS has plumbers at 504,500 employed in 2024, with about 44,000 openings a year through 2034.

But if you are underwriting a labour thesis, the gap between “a $33 billion problem” and “a $1.27 billion opportunity from 16,400 hires” is the gap between a crisis narrative and a hiring plan. Only one of them is in the source.

The trades shortage is the most-cited premise in home-services M&A right now. Which number is your thesis actually resting on — and have you opened the study behind it?
