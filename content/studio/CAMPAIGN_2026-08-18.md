### P-1 · Tue Aug 18, today · TEXT — The multiple nobody publishes

*Pattern: Threshold · Personas: P1, P3, P4 · Pillar: Where the Return Comes From · Honors queue item Q17*

A sub-$10M EBITDA company trades near 5.5x. The same company inside a $100M platform trades near 10x.

That spread is the entire buy-and-build thesis. It is also not automatic.

The gradient, from GF Data's sponsored-deal series: sub-$10M TEV around 5.5x, $10–25M around 5.9x, $100–250M around 10.0x. An add-on earns the platform multiple only when it becomes those things. And it becomes those things — or doesn't — in the eighteen months after the wire clears: in technician retention, in customers who were loyal to a person rather than a company, in add-backs that turn out not to recur.

The multiple you exit at is set by what you integrate, not what you sign.

The tailwind is real. The TAM is marketing. The edge is skepticism.

What's your fastest tell — before the QoE — that a founder-owned shop has value that actually transfers?

### P-6 · Tue Aug 25 · TEXT — The funnel is arithmetic

*Pattern: Threshold · Personas: P1, P3 · Pillar: Dead Deal Economics · Referral-safety note: funnel as math and cost, never "I can find you deals"*

The average searcher looks at 3,000 companies to buy one. The median investor reviews 80+ opportunities per investment.

Top-of-funnel isn't an effort problem. It's arithmetic.

IESE's international search fund study: the average searcher explored over 3,000 businesses, had real conversations with 159 owners, signed about four LOIs, and closed one. Stanford's data puts the first LOI around month seven. And in the Journal of Private Equity, Teten and Farmer found the median PE fund needed 3.1 investment-team members to close a single transaction in a year. (Teten runs a sourcing firm — the number serves his argument. It also matches the academic funnels, which is why I'll use it.)

Most teams answer this math with intensity. More outreach, more conferences, longer hours. Coverage barely moves — the median firm still sees a fraction of the deals inside its own box.

The expensive failure isn't the deal you never saw.

It's the one you did see. Ninety days of exclusivity, a $40K QoE, three site visits — mispriced, because funnel exhaustion had set in and the team needed something to close. Scarcity at the top of the funnel quietly becomes optimism at the bottom. That optimism is what you overpay with. And it's what integration inherits.

Capacity discipline beats intensity. The funnel is math; staff it like math.

For anyone running an add-on program: how many closed deals per sourcing seat did you actually get last year?
